Structural silicone glazing (SSG) explained for commercial projects

SSG is the system behind every flush glass facade you see on Class A office and premium hospitality buildings. There are no visible exterior pressure plates — the glass is bonded to the aluminum frame with structural-grade silicone that carries all wind and gravity loads. When it is detailed, inspected, and QC-tested correctly, it performs for decades. When it is not, it fails quietly and expensively.

By Connor Walsh · President, American Commercial Glass · FAA Pilot · Published May 13, 2026

The short answer

Structural silicone glazing (SSG) bonds glass to an aluminum frame with two-component structural-grade silicone — no exterior pressure plates, no visible mullion caps. The result is a flush facade.

What structural silicone glazing is and how it works

The system concept

In conventional captured glazing, the glass unit sits in a pocket in the aluminum frame and is held in place by an exterior pressure plate — an aluminum cap secured to the mullion with screws at regular intervals. The silicone between the glass edge and frame is a weatherseal, not a structural element. Remove the pressure plate and the glass is no longer retained.

In SSG, the pressure plate is eliminated on one or more sides of the glass unit. The glass is bonded directly to the aluminum frame using two-component structural silicone that cures to a rubber-like material capable of carrying tensile and shear loads. The silicone bite — the depth and width of the bonded contact between silicone and glass — is sized by calculation to carry the wind load and the dead weight of the glass to the frame, and through the frame to the structure. From the exterior, you see only glass, silicone joint lines, and — on properly detailed SSG — no aluminum whatsoever at the glass plane.

Two-sided, three-sided, and four-sided SSG

SSG can be applied to one, two, three, or four sides of the glass unit. The most common commercial configuration is two-sided SSG, where the vertical mullion sides are silicone-bonded and the horizontal (head and sill) sides are conventionally captured. This allows the glass to be installed and glazed from the interior for the horizontal members while the exterior face remains flush at the mullions. Four-sided SSG — all edges bonded — creates a fully flush appearance with no aluminum visible at any joint, which is the aesthetic driver for high-end curtainwall on premium projects.

The engineering and QC requirements increase with each additional SSG side. Four-sided SSG requires the silicone to carry dead load (glass weight) in shear, which demands verified bite width, verified silicone modulus, and destructive testing of production samples to ensure the bond will hold the glass dead load over the system's service life.

Silicone bite calculations

What "bite" means

The bite is the dimension of the silicone bead in contact with the glass edge — specifically the width (contact depth against the glass face) and the thickness (gap between the glass edge and the aluminum frame). Bite calculations determine the minimum dimensions of the silicone joint required to carry the design loads with an adequate factor of safety.

The calculation method

Structural silicone bite is calculated per ASTM C1184, ASTM C1369, and the silicone manufacturer's application engineering guidelines. The governing inputs are the design wind pressure (positive and negative), the glass weight, the glass unit dimensions, and the allowable stress of the silicone sealant product. For each SSG side, the required bite width is derived from the tributary wind load on that panel divided by the design tensile or shear strength of the silicone per unit length of bite, with the factor of safety specified by the standard (see the current ASTM standards and manufacturer application engineering documents for current values).

Minimum bite widths for structural silicone are typically in the range of 6mm to 38mm depending on panel size, design pressure, and silicone product — see the silicone manufacturer's technical data sheets and application engineering for current project-specific calculations. Bite calculations are the responsibility of the curtainwall system engineer and the silicone manufacturer's application engineering team. ACG coordinates the bite calculation review with the silicone manufacturer on every SSG project before fabrication begins.

Why bite dimensions cannot be field-adjusted

The silicone bite is applied in the fabrication shop — the glass is set in the frame, the gap is verified, and the sealant is injected and tooled in a controlled environment. Once cured, the bite dimension is fixed. There is no field adjustment. This is why the frame fabrication tolerances and the glass edge-to-frame gap must be controlled tightly in the shop. A glass unit that arrives with the wrong edge-to-frame clearance cannot simply be "shimmed up" — it needs to be reglazed with the correct gap, which means scraping the cured silicone off the aluminum, cleaning the substrate, and starting over. Fabrication quality control at the shop stage prevents this.

Silicone products used in SSG

Two-component vs one-component silicone

Structural silicone sealants for SSG are available as one-component and two-component formulations. One-component silicones cure by moisture absorption from the air — cure progresses from the exposed surface inward. For thick cross-sections (structural bite widths), moisture cure is slow and the through-cure depth per day is limited. Two-component silicones cure by chemical reaction between a base compound and a catalyst — cure is not moisture-dependent and proceeds at a predictable rate throughout the bead cross-section regardless of thickness. For structural applications where bite width requires full through-cure before load is applied, two-component silicone is the standard choice.

Dow structural silicone products

The most commonly specified two-component structural silicones on commercial curtainwall in the United States are Dow Corning products. Dow 983 SG is a two-component, black silicone sealant formulated specifically for SSG structural applications. Dow 995 is a two-component structural silicone available in black and neutral, widely specified on projects where the joint color needs to match the glass edge or where the owner prefers a neutral joint. Dow 795 is a one-component high-modulus silicone used primarily for weathersealing and secondary seals in SSG systems rather than as the primary structural sealant — it is sometimes incorrectly specified as the structural bond in lower-budget projects, which is a misapplication of the product.

Sika (SikaGlaze series) and Tremco (Spectrem series) also manufacture two-component structural silicones that are specified on commercial SSG projects. Product selection should be based on the curtainwall system engineer's recommendation and the substrate compatibility testing results for the specific aluminum alloy and surface treatment on the project. Always obtain a written compatibility and adhesion test from the silicone manufacturer for the actual substrates in the project — test reports from other projects are not transferable.

Silicone shelf life and batch control

Two-component silicone sealants have a shelf life — typically 12 to 18 months from date of manufacture at controlled storage temperatures (see manufacturer data sheets for current shelf life specifications). Sealant used beyond its shelf life may not achieve the rated strength, regardless of how it looks or flows. On SSG projects, the fabrication shop should document the batch number, date of manufacture, and date of application for every tube or drum used, keyed to the panel units it was used on. This documentation is part of the project's quality record and may be required for warranty compliance or for forensic investigation of any future failure.

Quality control and testing requirements

Butterfly test (peel test)

The primary in-process quality control test for SSG is the butterfly peel test, also called the "H-piece" test or bond strength test. A coupon is fabricated alongside the production units — the same aluminum substrate, same silicone batch, same application procedure, same cure conditions. After the cure period, the coupon is cut and peeled — the silicone is pulled from the substrate and the failure mode is examined. A passing result shows cohesive failure (the silicone tears within itself), not adhesive failure (the silicone peels cleanly off the aluminum surface). Adhesive failure indicates a surface preparation problem: contamination, insufficient primer, incompatible coating, or expired silicone.

The butterfly test is typically performed at a frequency of one test per production run, per day, or per silicone batch change — confirm the required frequency with the silicone manufacturer and the project specification. Results are documented and retained as part of the project quality record. A failed butterfly test stops production until the cause is identified and the substrate preparation procedure is corrected.

Oven cure test and extended cure verification

For two-component silicones, the snap cure test (or "snap time" test) is performed on each mixed batch to verify that the catalyst ratio is correct and the silicone will cure within the expected time window. A correct snap time indicates proper mixing ratio. An incorrect snap time — too fast or too slow — means the batch is out of ratio and should be discarded. This test takes two minutes and is performed at the point of application before the batch is used.

Inspector access and documentation

SSG fabrication requires special inspection — typically a third-party special inspector or the manufacturer's representative witnessing the silicone application process, documenting batch numbers, snap cure times, butterfly test results, and application conditions (temperature, humidity). This documentation package is typically required by the engineer of record and may be required by the AHJ as part of the special inspection plan. Budget for special inspection in the SSG project schedule — it is not optional on a commercial project and it takes time to coordinate.

When SSG is the right choice

SSG is the right choice when the owner and architect have a specific facade aesthetic that requires a flush glass face — no aluminum caps interrupting the glass plane. On a Class A office building where the curtainwall is visible from the street and the leasing materials show a continuous glass facade, SSG is the only system that delivers the expected appearance. On projects where the aesthetic is secondary and the priority is cost control, maintainability, and straightforward inspection, captured glazing is the defensible choice and a smaller specification risk.

Frequently asked questions

What is structural silicone glazing (SSG)?

SSG is a curtainwall or window wall system where the glass is bonded to the aluminum frame using two-component structural-grade silicone adhesive rather than mechanical pressure plates. The silicone carries all wind loads and the dead weight of the glass. The result is a flush exterior face with no visible mullion caps. It is the standard system for Class A commercial facades and premium hospitality curtainwall.

Is structural silicone glazing durable?

Properly applied two-component structural silicone — using products such as Dow 983 or 995, with correct surface preparation, verified bite dimensions, and butterfly test QC — has demonstrated service lives exceeding 30 years in field conditions. The risk is not the silicone product itself but the application process. Insufficient surface preparation and incorrect mixing ratios are the root causes of SSG failures. Quality control testing at the fabrication stage is the mechanism for catching these problems before they become field failures.

What is the difference between SSG and captured glazing?

In captured glazing, the glass is held by aluminum pressure plates screwed to the mullion — the silicone is a weatherseal only. In SSG, structural silicone is the only retainer on the SSG sides — no exterior pressure plates. The visual result is a flush glass face. Structurally, captured systems are more tolerant of silicone quality variation because the mechanical fasteners carry the load. SSG depends entirely on the silicone bond quality.

What silicone products are used for SSG?

The most commonly specified two-component structural silicones are Dow 983 SG (black) and Dow 995 (black or neutral). Two-component sealants are preferred because their cure is driven by chemical reaction, not moisture exposure, giving predictable through-cure in thick bite cross-sections. Sika and Tremco also manufacture two-component structural silicones. Product selection requires compatibility and adhesion testing against the specific aluminum alloy and surface treatment on the project — test reports from other projects are not transferable.
