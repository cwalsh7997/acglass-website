AAMA 502 field water testing for installed assemblies

AAMA 502 is the test that confirms your installed windows, storefront, or curtainwall actually performs the way the NOA says it should. In Florida HVHZ, it is frequently required by AHJs before certificate of occupancy. When a GC discovers this at closeout instead of permit application, it costs weeks. Here is everything you need to know to plan it correctly from the start.

By Connor Walsh · President, American Commercial Glass · FL CGC #1531993 · FAA Pilot · Published May 13, 2026

The short answer

AAMA 502 is field water resistance testing of installed window and glazing assemblies using a controlled water chamber and pressure differential. Florida HVHZ AHJs commonly require it before certificate of occupancy. Most failures are at perimeter sealant joints, not the glass or frame.

What AAMA 502 is and what it tests

The standard and its purpose

AAMA 502 — "Voluntary Specification for Field Testing of Newly Installed Fenestration Products" — is the American Architectural Manufacturers Association standard that defines how to conduct water resistance testing on fenestration assemblies after they are installed in a building. It is a field standard, not a laboratory standard. The product was tested in a laboratory as part of its NOA or FPA certification; AAMA 502 testing verifies that the installation — the anchoring, sealant application, weep hole configuration, and perimeter caulking — has been executed in a way that achieves the product's certified performance in the field.

AAMA 502 is separate from the AAMA 501 family of standards. AAMA 501 covers laboratory water penetration testing (501.1), field spray diagnostic testing (501.2), post-installation field testing with a pressure differential (501.4), and thermal cycling (501.5). AAMA 502 specifically addresses the full-chamber field test of installed assemblies. See our companion guide on AAMA 501 explained for the full comparison between these standards.

What the test actually measures

AAMA 502 applies a measured quantity of water to the exterior face of the installed assembly under a calibrated pressure differential. The pressure differential simulates the wind load that drives rain against the building face during a storm event — the combination of water volume and air pressure is what creates water infiltration risk. A unit that holds water out at zero pressure may fail at elevated pressure because the pressure differential pushes water through gaps that would drain freely under static conditions.

The test pressure for AAMA 502 is specified in the standard as a function of the product's water resistance rating — typically a percentage of the product's rated design pressure. For commercial fenestration with higher design pressure ratings (common in HVHZ), the field test pressure is correspondingly higher. See the current edition of AAMA 502 for the exact test pressure calculation method, as these parameters may be updated between editions.

How the test is performed — step by step

Test setup

A water chamber — a sealed frame assembly with a transparent front panel — is mounted to the exterior face of the installed unit and sealed at the perimeter. The chamber covers the full face of the unit and creates an airtight seal between the chamber and the frame perimeter. Water is introduced at the specified flow rate through nozzles inside the chamber while air pressure is applied through a port at the chamber base or side. A calibrated pressure gauge monitors the differential throughout the test duration.

The chamber must seal completely to the frame for the test to be valid. If the chamber leaks air at the perimeter seal, the test pressure cannot be maintained and the test is invalid — not a pass or fail, but a void test requiring a re-setup. On brick veneer or punched openings with irregular perimeter conditions, achieving a good chamber seal can require additional preparation by the contractor before the testing firm arrives.

Test duration and pass/fail criteria

AAMA 502 specifies the test duration (see the current AAMA 502 edition for the exact duration requirement). During the test, the interior of the unit is monitored for water infiltration. The pass/fail criterion is no uncontrolled water infiltration — meaning no water appearing on the interior side of the assembly at locations that are not designed drainage paths. A drip at a weep hole that exits to the exterior is not a failure; water appearing at the interior sill or at the head is a failure. The testing technician documents the exact location and time of any observed infiltration during the test run.

Mockup testing vs installed (in-situ) testing

Mockup testing

A mockup test is performed on a representative assembly built specifically for testing — typically a two-bay or three-bay section of curtainwall or a representative window unit, constructed by the glazing subcontractor using the same product, installation procedure, and sealant specified for the project. The mockup is tested before full installation begins. The purpose is to validate that the specified system and installation method will pass AAMA 502 before the glazing contractor has installed the entire project scope.

On large curtainwall projects — typically those above a certain dollar value or square footage threshold — the contract and the specification commonly require a mockup test. The timing is critical: the mockup should be constructed and tested during the shop drawing review and fabrication period, not after installation has begun. If the mockup fails and the failure requires a detail change — a different sealant configuration, a modified weep hole pattern, a different perimeter anchor detail — you want to catch that before the first unit goes into the building.

Installed (in-situ) testing

In-situ testing is performed on the actual production units after installation is complete. The AHJ and the specification typically define the testing sample — a percentage of the total unit count, or a minimum number of units including specific locations (corner units, units at the highest floor, units with the most complex perimeter conditions). The standard selection methodology under AAMA 502 is random selection with overweighting toward the most complex or highest-risk locations.

Florida AHJ requirements in HVHZ

Why HVHZ AHJs require AAMA 502

In Miami-Dade and Broward counties, the stakes of a failed fenestration installation are higher than in most jurisdictions. A building envelope failure during a hurricane event exposes occupants and interiors to immediate and severe damage. The NOA certification system ensures the product is capable of the required performance; AAMA 502 field testing ensures the installation achieved that performance. Miami-Dade's building department and the Broward County Building Division both have provisions for requiring AAMA 502 testing as a closeout condition on commercial glazing projects.

The specific trigger — what project characteristics cause an AHJ to require AAMA 502 — varies by building department and by the individual plan reviewer. There is no single statewide rule that defines when AAMA 502 is required versus when it is optional. The factors that commonly trigger an AAMA 502 requirement include: project size (total square footage of glazing above a threshold), building height, the presence of HVHZ curtainwall or storefront on a commercial occupancy, and whether the permit set included a specific testing requirement in the construction documents. On any HVHZ commercial project with significant glazing scope, ask the building department at permit application whether AAMA 502 testing will be required before CO. Do not wait until closeout.

What drives AAMA 502 testing cost

AAMA 502 testing is performed by third-party testing firms — not the glazing contractor and not the building inspector. The firm mobilizes to the jobsite with the testing equipment, performs the tests, and provides a written report with results for each unit tested. Testing cost varies significantly with scope:

What happens when a unit fails the AAMA 502 test

Understanding what failure actually means

A failed unit does not mean the glass is defective or the product is wrong for the project. AAMA 502 failures on commercial glazing installations are almost always installation failures — not product failures. The product passed its NOA testing. The failure is in how the unit was installed in the field: incomplete sealant coverage at a corner joint, a weep hole cover installed upside down, a perimeter caulk joint with a void at a transition, or a setting block placed incorrectly that allows water to pool at the sill.

The testing technician's report will identify the location where water infiltration was observed. In most cases, the failure location is at the perimeter sealant interface — the joint between the window or storefront frame and the surrounding substrate (block, concrete, stucco, or curtainwall metal). On the jobsite this matters because the fix is almost always a sealant correction — a targeted cut, clean, and re-caulk at the failure location — not a glass replacement or a system change.

The failure-correction-retest cycle

The standard protocol after a failure: the testing firm documents the failure with photographs and a written report identifying the failure location. The glazing contractor makes the correction — typically within 1–5 business days for a sealant fix. The testing firm returns and re-tests the corrected unit. A passing result on the re-test is documented in a revised or supplemental report. The corrected and passing report is submitted to the AHJ to clear the testing requirement for that unit.

If the initial test finds failures on multiple units, the failure pattern informs whether the issue is systematic or isolated. Multiple failures at the same detail — for example, all corner conditions at the head — indicate a systematic installation error that needs to be corrected on all units, not just the tested ones. In this case, a broader sample of units may need correction and re-testing, and the schedule impact is larger. The earlier the mockup test catches this pattern, the smaller the impact on the production installation.

Schedule planning: where projects get into trouble

The four scheduling mistakes we see repeatedly

First: AAMA 502 testing is not in the schedule at all. The GC's closeout schedule goes directly from "glazing complete" to "certificate of occupancy." There is no time allocated for testing coordination, testing, failure correction, and re-test. When the AHJ conditions CO on AAMA 502 results, the project adds 2–5 weeks to the closeout timeline at the worst possible time.

Second: the testing firm is not contracted until after glazing is complete. Good AAMA 502 testing firms are booked in advance. Calling them three days before you need them produces either a schedule delay waiting for availability or a premium mobilization cost for expedited scheduling.

Third: the mockup test is scheduled too late. If the mockup is tested during the final weeks of installation rather than before installation begins, a mockup failure requires correcting already-installed production units. The mockup's value — catching systematic installation errors before production — is only realized if it is scheduled and completed before significant production installation has occurred.

Fourth: the AHJ's specific AAMA 502 requirement was never confirmed. The GC finds out at CO application that the building department requires AAMA 502 testing. The testing firm is not contracted. The schedule adds weeks and the owner is not happy. Confirm the AAMA 502 requirement with the AHJ at permit application and include it in the project schedule from day one.

Frequently asked questions

What is AAMA 502?

AAMA 502 is the American Architectural Manufacturers Association standard for field water resistance testing of installed windows, doors, storefronts, and curtainwall assemblies. A controlled water chamber is sealed to the installed unit; water is applied at a specified rate while a calibrated pressure differential is maintained. It verifies that the installed assembly performs to its certified water resistance level in field conditions.

When is AAMA 502 required?

AAMA 502 is required when the AHJ mandates it as a CO condition, when the project specification requires it, or when the contract includes a systematic post-installation water test. In Florida HVHZ, many AHJs require AAMA 502 on commercial glazing projects of significant scale. Confirm the requirement with the building department at permit application — not at closeout.

What happens if an AAMA 502 test fails?

A failure does not mean removing the unit. The testing firm identifies the failure location — typically a sealant joint, weep hole cover, or perimeter caulk. The contractor makes the correction, and the unit is re-tested. A passing re-test result is documented and submitted to the AHJ. Build 2–4 weeks of schedule float in the closeout phase for this correction-retest cycle in case failures occur.

What is the difference between mockup and installed testing?

Mockup testing is performed on a representative assembly built before full installation begins, to validate the system and installation method before production. Installed (in-situ) testing is performed on the actual production units after installation is complete. On large curtainwall projects, both phases are commonly required. The mockup's value is only realized if it is completed before production installation is underway.
