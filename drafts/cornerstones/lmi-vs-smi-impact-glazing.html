Large Missile Impact (LMI) vs Small Missile Impact (SMI) — when each applies

Two missile classifications, one threshold that determines which you need: 30 feet above grade. Get this wrong in the specification and the NOA cover sheet will not match the project conditions. On the jobsite this matters because the inspector reads the NOA cover sheet before they read anything else.

By Connor Walsh · President, American Commercial Glass · FL CGC #1531993 · FAA Pilot · Published May 13, 2026

The short answer

LMI (Large Missile Impact) requires a 9-pound 2x4 fired at 50 ft/sec — required below 30 feet of elevation. SMI (Small Missile Impact) uses 30 steel balls at 130 ft/sec — required at and above 30 feet. An LMI-rated assembly always satisfies an SMI requirement. The reverse is not true.

The two missile classifications defined

Large Missile Impact (LMI)

LMI is the high-threat impact classification. The standard projectile is a nominal 2x4 timber stud (9 pounds) fired end-on at the glazing assembly. The test velocity under ASTM E1996 is 50 feet per second — that is approximately 34 miles per hour for the lumber alone, which does not convey the force involved because the 9-pound mass delivers a substantial kinetic energy load concentrated at a small area. The assembly must show no penetration and no opening after two impacts at specified locations on the specimen.

The physical rationale is straightforward: below 30 feet of elevation, wind-blown lumber from construction sites, dumpsters, and surrounding structures is a credible threat. After Hurricane Andrew, the debris field analysis showed that large lumber was responsible for a disproportionate share of envelope failures on low-rise residential and commercial buildings. LMI testing was calibrated to represent that threat scenario.

Small Missile Impact (SMI)

SMI addresses the elevated-floor threat profile. At heights above 30 feet, large lumber debris is less likely to remain airborne long enough to reach the building face at damaging velocity. The relevant threat shifts to roofing gravel, roof ballast, and smaller wind-carried debris. The SMI test uses 30 steel balls — each approximately 2 grams — fired at the assembly at 130 feet per second (approximately 89 mph). The aggregate impact energy is distributed across the assembly face, testing the glazing's resistance to multiple simultaneous small-body impacts rather than one concentrated large body.

SMI is a less demanding classification than LMI in practical terms. An assembly designed and tested to LMI will pass SMI — the LMI projectile carries more threat. This is why LMI-rated assemblies are universally acceptable where SMI is the minimum code requirement.

The 30-foot elevation threshold — what it means and how to measure it

How the 30-foot measurement is defined

The 30-foot threshold is measured from the ground floor finished floor elevation (or grade, depending on which reference the AHJ applies) to the sill of the opening in question. The Florida Building Code and ASTM E1996 both use a height-above-grade reference. In practice, "grade" is the grade established by the permitted site plan at the base of the building. This matters on projects where the building sits on a podium, has a parking structure at grade, or where grade changes around the perimeter.

On a typical three-story commercial building with a 14-foot ground floor, the second floor sill will be above 14 feet — still LMI territory. A third floor sill in the 28–30-foot range may straddle the threshold depending on floor-to-floor heights. On mixed-use buildings with double-height ground floors, the transition happens at a lower story count than people expect.

What "at 30 feet" means in practice

ASTM E1996 defines the boundary as "at or above 30 feet." Openings with sills exactly at 30 feet are in the SMI zone. The issue on real projects is when the engineer of record calculates floor-to-floor heights and the sills fall at 29 feet 6 inches versus 30 feet 6 inches. We have seen shop drawings challenged during permit review because the elevation takeoff did not match the design pressures and missile ratings on the submittal.

On taller buildings — mid-rise and high-rise — every floor above the ground is above 30 feet, so the entire glazing scope is SMI minimum. This is the common case for Class A commercial office and residential towers. The LMI requirement is concentrated on the first two to three floors of most commercial buildings.

Mixed-elevation buildings

On a building where some floors require LMI and some require SMI, you have a choice: specify LMI for the entire building, or manage two separate glazing specifications and NOA covers. In most cases, when the building elevation mix is close to the threshold, we recommend using a single LMI-rated product across all floors. The cost premium of LMI over SMI is typically small relative to the value engineering savings from a single NOA configuration and simplified submittal. Confirm the cost differential with your glazing contractor and manufacturer rep — it varies by product line and project scale.

ASTM E1996 and Florida TAS protocols

The national standard: ASTM E1996

ASTM E1996 is the national standard specification for performance of exterior windows, curtain walls, doors, and impact protective systems impacted by windborne debris in hurricanes. It defines missile levels, test requirements, and performance criteria for both LMI and SMI. ASTM E1886 is the companion test method that covers the actual impact testing procedure and the cyclic pressure follow-on test.

ASTM E1996 defines multiple missile levels — Levels A, B, C, D, and E — but for Florida commercial work, the operative categories are the 9-pound 2x4 (Large Missile, corresponding to ASTM Level E for most applications) and the small steel balls (Small Missile). For Florida HVHZ, the TAS (Test Application Standards) are the controlling standards, not ASTM directly — but the TAS standards were developed in alignment with ASTM and reference the same projectile specifications. See the Florida Building Code and Miami-Dade Product Control for the current TAS edition applicable to your project.

Florida TAS 201 and TAS 202

Florida Test Application Standard 201 covers large missile impact testing for HVHZ. TAS 202 covers small missile impact testing. TAS 203 is the cyclic wind pressure test that follows both TAS 201 and TAS 202 — the assembly must complete the impact test and then immediately go through 9,000 positive and negative pressure cycles without failure. The cyclic test is not optional and it is not performed on a separate specimen — it is performed on the same specimen that took the missile impacts. This is a rigorous sequence that replicates how an assembly performs after taking hurricane debris and then sustaining hours of cyclic wind loading.

Water infiltration testing (TAS 202.1 or ASTM E331) is also part of the NOA approval sequence. The assembly tested for impact is also the assembly tested for water — there is no separate "water-only" NOA for HVHZ. The full system — frame, glass type, thickness, interlayer, anchor pattern, sealant specification — is certified as a unit.

Outside Florida: wind-borne debris regions

Outside HVHZ, ASTM E1996 governs in other wind-borne debris regions defined by ASCE 7. The 30-foot LMI/SMI threshold is consistent with the ASTM standard nationally. If you are working on a non-Florida coastal project with high design wind speeds, ASTM E1996 is the reference — confirm with the local building code and engineer of record for the exact missile classification requirements. Florida's TAS protocols are more stringent in some respects than the ASTM minimum, so Florida-NOA-listed products will typically exceed what other jurisdictions require.

Reading LMI and SMI designation on an NOA cover sheet

Where to look on the NOA document

A Miami-Dade NOA is a multi-page document. The cover sheet lists the product name, manufacturer, NOA number, issue date, and expiration date. The scope section on page one or two states explicitly whether the product is approved for Large Missile Impact, Small Missile Impact, or both. You will see language such as "Approved for use in HVHZ for openings protected against Large Missile Impact per TAS 201" or "Rated for Small Missile Impact (TAS 202) for openings above 30 feet."

Some NOAs cover both missile levels within the same document, with a table mapping specific frame/glass/thickness combinations to their respective missile ratings. This is important: do not assume that because the NOA header says "Large Missile Impact," every configuration in the NOA is LMI-rated. An NOA for a manufacturer's storefront line may have certain frame depths rated LMI and lighter-duty configurations rated SMI only. Verify the specific combination you are specifying against the table in the NOA — not just the cover sheet summary.

What to check before submitting for permit

On every project, our submittal package includes a cross-reference matrix: for each opening on the drawing, we note the elevation (to determine LMI vs SMI), the NOA number, the specific configuration from the NOA that covers that opening, and the NOA expiration date. This document goes to the AHJ with the shop drawings. It answers the first question any reviewer will ask: "Does this NOA configuration cover this opening at this elevation?" When the answer is documented in advance, permit review is faster and comment cycles are shorter.

FBC HVHZ requirements for missile impact glazing

The code hierarchy

In HVHZ (Miami-Dade and Broward counties), the Florida Building Code requires Miami-Dade NOA-listed assemblies for all exterior openings. The NOA system, administered by Miami-Dade Product Control, is the vehicle through which LMI and SMI compliance is documented. There is no alternative path to HVHZ glazing approval — Florida Product Approval (FPA) without a Miami-Dade NOA is insufficient for HVHZ.

Outside HVHZ but inside a wind-borne debris region (which covers much of coastal Florida), the FPA system is the governing approval mechanism. FPA products are tested under ASTM E1996 and ASTM E1886, which use the same LMI/SMI threshold. The 30-foot elevation rule applies equally under FPA and NOA. The difference is the testing rigor and the approval authority — Miami-Dade NOA is more stringent. See our guide on HVHZ glazing requirements and the Florida Product Approval vs NOA comparison for more detail.

How elevation is verified in the field

The AHJ inspector verifies missile classification compliance by checking that the product label on the installed unit matches the NOA, and that the NOA covers the appropriate missile level for the floor where the unit is installed. On projects where LMI is required on lower floors and SMI on upper floors, the inspector will check that the LMI-rated units are actually installed below 30 feet and that SMI-only units are not installed in LMI-required locations.

On the jobsite, this matters in the sequencing of product delivery and installation. If LMI and SMI products are from the same manufacturer and product line but different part numbers, the warehouse and field team need a clear reference — unit by unit — for which product goes where. We use floor-coded shop drawing tags on every unit and a delivery manifest that matches the shop drawing numbering. This is the only way to guarantee the right product is installed at the right elevation on a project with more than a handful of units.

Common specification errors — and how they cause permit rejections

Specifying SMI-only for all floors without checking elevation

This is the most common error we see from out-of-state design teams working on Florida projects for the first time. The specification section lists a product and references the manufacturer's NOA, but the NOA covers SMI only. The building has three floors of residential or retail at grade — all in LMI territory. The permit review catches it, the spec has to be revised, and the procurement clock restarts. Check every opening's elevation against the missile classification before submitting the specification to bid.

Assuming a product has LMI when it only has SMI

Not all products in a manufacturer's line have identical NOA coverage. A manufacturer may have an LMI-rated product for one frame series and an SMI-only product for a lighter series. If a design team specifies the lighter series based on aesthetic or cost preference without checking the NOA missile level, the spec may call out a product that cannot be used on lower floors. This is particularly common with slimline storefront systems — the narrow sightline versions often carry SMI-only ratings because the frame geometry does not accommodate the deeper glass bite required for LMI certification.

Using the wrong elevation reference point

On sites with significant grade changes, the 30-foot reference can be ambiguous. A building on a sloped site may have one face of the first floor at grade and the opposite face 8 feet above grade on the downhill side. The missile classification should be determined per opening based on the actual elevation of the sill above grade at that face of the building, per the site survey and permitted grade plan. Do not apply a single floor-height proxy across all faces of a building on an irregular site.

Failing to document the LMI/SMI split in the submittal

Even when the products are correctly specified, a submittal that does not clearly document which openings are LMI and which are SMI — and which NOA configuration covers each — will generate a comment from the permit reviewer. The burden of demonstrating compliance is on the submitter. A comment cycle that asks "please confirm the missile level of each opening" adds 2–4 weeks to permit review on a complex project. Front-load the documentation.

Frequently asked questions

What is Large Missile Impact (LMI)?

LMI is the impact resistance classification for glazing subject to wind-borne lumber and large debris. Under ASTM E1996 and Florida TAS 201, the test uses a 9-pound 2x4 timber stud fired at 50 feet per second. The assembly must resist penetration and then pass 9,000 cyclic pressure cycles (TAS 203). LMI is required for all fenestration below 30 feet of elevation in HVHZ and wind-borne debris regions.

What is Small Missile Impact (SMI)?

SMI simulates the threat of wind-blown gravel, roof ballast, and small debris at elevation. Under ASTM E1996 and Florida TAS 202, 30 steel balls (approximately 2 grams each) are fired at 130 feet per second. The rationale is that large lumber is less likely to become airborne at damaging velocity above 30 feet. SMI is required at and above 30 feet above grade.

When do I need LMI vs SMI?

The threshold is the sill elevation of the opening measured from grade. Below 30 feet: LMI required. At 30 feet and above: SMI minimum. In HVHZ, both thresholds apply under Florida TAS 201 and TAS 202. Outside HVHZ in wind-borne debris regions, the same threshold applies under ASTM E1996. Confirm with the engineer of record and the local AHJ — grade reference and floor height measurement methodology can vary by jurisdiction.

Can I substitute LMI-rated glazing where SMI is required?

Yes. LMI is the more demanding classification, so an LMI-rated assembly satisfies an SMI requirement. The reverse is not true: SMI-only assemblies cannot be used in LMI-required locations. On buildings with mixed-elevation glazing, using a single LMI-rated product throughout often simplifies procurement, submittal documentation, and field installation sequencing.

How is missile impact testing actually performed?

Testing is conducted at accredited laboratories. For LMI, a 9-pound 2x4 is fired end-on at two locations on the specimen at 50 ft/sec. For SMI, 30 steel balls are fired at 130 ft/sec. The assembly must show no penetration. It then undergoes 9,000 positive and negative pressure cycles (TAS 203 / ASTM E1886) on the same specimen. The entire assembly — frame, glass, interlayer, anchors, sealant — is tested as a unit. Substituting any component after testing invalidates the approval.

Where does the LMI/SMI designation appear on the NOA?

On a Miami-Dade NOA, the missile classification appears on the cover sheet and in the scope section. Some NOAs cover both classifications, with individual frame/glass combinations mapped to their respective levels in an internal table. Verify the specific combination you are specifying — not just the overall NOA document — against the missile level table before submitting for permit.
