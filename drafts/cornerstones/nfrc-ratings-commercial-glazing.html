NFRC ratings explained for commercial glazing — U-factor, SHGC, VT, AL

Four numbers determine whether a commercial glazing assembly passes energy code compliance and contributes to LEED: U-factor, SHGC, visible transmittance, and air leakage. Florida's climate makes the SHGC number the most consequential — but pushing SHGC down hard trades against the visible light you want inside the building. Here is how to read these ratings and make informed specification decisions.

By Connor Walsh · President, American Commercial Glass · FL CGC #1531993 · FAA Pilot · Published May 13, 2026

The short answer

NFRC rates four energy performance metrics for commercial glazing: U-factor (heat flow, lower = better), SHGC (solar gain, lower = less heat in Florida), VT (visible light, higher = more daylight), and AL (air leakage, lower = tighter assembly). Florida energy code requires NFRC-certified values for code compliance, and ASHRAE 90.1 prescriptive tables set the maximum U-factor and SHGC by climate zone and window-to-wall ratio.

What NFRC is and what it certifies

The organization and its role

The National Fenestration Rating Council is a nonprofit organization that develops energy performance standards and certification programs for windows, doors, skylights, and fenestration products. NFRC does not manufacture products — it operates a certification system that independent manufacturers and laboratories use to verify and label the energy performance of their products. The NFRC certification label and the NFRC Certified Products Directory (at nfrc.org) are the authoritative sources for energy performance data on certified products.

NFRC certification is referenced by the major energy codes — ASHRAE 90.1, the International Energy Conservation Code (IECC), and the Florida Building Code energy provisions — as the acceptable method of demonstrating fenestration energy performance compliance. A commercial project that uses NFRC-certified values in the energy compliance pathway gets to use the actual certified performance numbers. A project using non-NFRC values must use the default, less favorable values specified in the energy code, which are typically penalizing assumptions intended to discourage the use of uncertified products.

What NFRC labels show

An NFRC label — either a physical label on the product unit or a certification entry in the NFRC directory — shows four primary values for commercial fenestration: U-factor, SHGC, VT, and where tested, AL. The values are whole-assembly ratings — they include the frame, the glass, the spacer system, and any edge effects. They are not center-of-glass values. This distinction matters because a glass manufacturer's data sheet may show center-of-glass U-factor and SHGC values that are significantly better than the NFRC whole-assembly values. Energy code compliance uses whole-assembly values — center-of-glass values are not acceptable substitutes for code compliance purposes.

U-factor — thermal insulation performance

What U-factor measures

U-factor is the overall heat transfer coefficient of the fenestration assembly — it measures the rate at which non-solar heat flows through the unit in BTU per hour per square foot per degree Fahrenheit temperature difference. A U-factor of 0.30 means 0.30 BTU of heat flows through each square foot of assembly for each degree Fahrenheit of temperature difference between inside and outside. Lower U-factor = better thermal insulation = less heat transfer.

U-factor is the inverse of R-value (which is more familiar in the opaque wall and roof insulation context). R = 1/U. A U-0.30 assembly has an R-value of approximately 3.3 — far lower than the R-19 or R-30 you would see in an insulated wall. Glazing is not a good thermal insulator compared to opaque walls regardless of how low the U-factor is. The energy code requirement for commercial fenestration U-factor is a practical floor, not an aspirational target — it represents the minimum thermal performance required to meet the building's overall energy budget.

SHGC — solar heat gain coefficient

What SHGC measures and why it matters more than U-factor in Florida

SHGC is the fraction of incident solar radiation that passes through the glazing assembly and enters the building interior as heat. An SHGC of 0.25 means 25% of the solar energy striking the glass becomes heat load inside the building. The remaining 75% is reflected or absorbed by the glass and re-radiated to the exterior.

In Florida's hot climate, SHGC has a larger impact on building energy use than U-factor for most commercial buildings. The heating load in Florida is minimal — the energy model is dominated by cooling loads. Solar gain through glazing is a major driver of cooling load, particularly for buildings with large glazing ratios (window-to-wall ratios above 40%), south and west-facing facades, and buildings in South Florida where direct solar radiation is intense year-round. A 0.05 reduction in SHGC on a large curtainwall facade has a larger annual energy cost impact than the same fractional improvement in U-factor.

SHGC ranges and what to specify in Florida

Commercial glazing products in Florida typically fall in the SHGC 0.20 to 0.45 range. Products with SHGC below 0.20 exist but carry significant visible light penalties and are typically specified only for specific solar control applications. The practical SHGC decision for Florida commercial work depends on facade orientation, the building's window-to-wall ratio, and the energy compliance pathway:

  • South and west facades with high WWR: Target SHGC 0.20–0.28 to minimize cooling load. This typically requires a high-performance spectrally selective low-e coating.
  • North facades and shaded locations: SHGC 0.30–0.40 range is acceptable; the solar gain contribution is lower and the daylight benefit is higher.
  • Code minimum approach (ASHRAE 90.1 prescriptive): Confirm the maximum SHGC for your climate zone and window-to-wall ratio from the current ASHRAE 90.1 table — values are updated with each code cycle.

VT — visible transmittance

What VT measures

Visible transmittance (VT) is the fraction of visible light (wavelengths approximately 380–780 nm) that passes through the glazing assembly. A VT of 0.40 means 40% of incident visible light enters the building. VT directly affects the quality of natural daylight in the space, the appearance of the facade from the interior, and glare conditions for occupants near the glass.

Higher VT means more daylight — beneficial for occupant comfort, circadian rhythm, and daylight contribution to reduced electric lighting. ASHRAE 90.1 and the IECC reference the "light-to-solar gain ratio" (LSG = VT/SHGC) as a quality metric for glazing. An LSG above 1.0 indicates that the glazing admits more visible light per unit of solar heat gain than clear glass — meaning the glass is doing useful spectral filtering work. High-performance commercial glazing in Florida should target an LSG of 1.2 or higher to balance solar control with daylight.

Typical VT ranges

Clear uncoated glass has a VT of approximately 0.87–0.90 for a 1/4-inch pane. Low-e coatings and insulating glass units reduce VT. High-performance solar control coatings with low SHGC (0.20–0.25) typically have VT values in the 0.40–0.55 range — roughly half the visible light of clear glass. Bronze and gray tinted glass can have VT values below 0.40 at the cost of reduced daylight and a darker interior appearance. Spectrally selective low-e coatings achieve the best LSG performance — low SHGC combined with relatively high VT — and are the standard choice for high-performance commercial facades in Florida.

AL — air leakage

What air leakage rating means

Air leakage (AL) is the rate of air flow through the fenestration assembly at a specified pressure differential, measured in cubic feet per minute per square foot of frame area (CFM/SF). Lower AL = tighter assembly = less infiltration of hot humid outside air (in the Florida summer cooling scenario). AL is measured and rated under NFRC 400 using ASTM E283 as the underlying test method.

AL is not the same as the water resistance or air infiltration ratings under AAMA testing. AAMA air infiltration testing (per AAMA/WDMA/CSA 101/I.S.2/A440) uses different test pressures and reports results in different units. The NFRC AL value is used specifically for energy code compliance via ASHRAE 90.1 or the IECC. For HVHZ and FPA products, the AAMA air infiltration testing is the compliance mechanism for the building envelope performance requirements.

Typical NFRC AL values for commercial fixed glazing are 0.02–0.04 CFM/SF. For operable windows and doors, AL values are higher — typically 0.1–0.3 CFM/SF — because operable assemblies inherently have more air leakage paths through the operating mechanism and weatherstripping. For energy code compliance, confirm the maximum AL allowed in the current edition of ASHRAE 90.1 or the FBC energy provisions for the specific product type.

Florida Building Code and ASHRAE 90.1 requirements

Florida's climate zones and why they matter for glazing specifications

Florida spans ASHRAE climate zones 1 (extreme south Florida — Miami-Dade, Monroe), 2 (most of the state including Broward, Palm Beach, Tampa, Fort Myers), and 3A (north Florida — Jacksonville, Tallahassee, Pensacola). Climate zone determines which prescriptive table in ASHRAE 90.1 applies. The prescriptive tables set maximum U-factor and SHGC by climate zone, building type (commercial vs residential), and window-to-wall ratio (WWR) tier.

The FBC Energy Conservation volume adopts ASHRAE 90.1 by reference. Confirm which edition of ASHRAE 90.1 is currently adopted by the FBC — Florida may be on a different adoption cycle than the current ASHRAE publication. The current edition of the FBC and the adopted ASHRAE 90.1 edition are the controlling documents. See the Florida Building Commission and ASHRAE for current values before finalizing a specification.

Prescriptive compliance path

The prescriptive path is the most common energy code compliance path for commercial glazing in Florida. The designer looks up the maximum allowable U-factor and SHGC for the project's climate zone and proposed WWR in the ASHRAE 90.1 prescriptive table, then specifies glazing products with NFRC-certified values that meet or exceed the requirements. Products must have NFRC certification — default code values (which are penalizing) apply if NFRC data is not available.

Performance path (energy modeling)

The performance path — an energy simulation showing that the proposed building as a whole meets or exceeds the energy budget of the prescriptive base case — allows trade-offs between glazing performance and other building envelope or system improvements. On projects where the WWR is high (above 40%) or where the prescriptive path is difficult to satisfy with the desired glazing product, the performance path via a building energy model (typically EnergyPlus or eQuest-based) is the compliance strategy. The energy modeler needs NFRC-certified U-factor and SHGC values for the glazing products — this is a coordination item between the glazing contractor, the architect, and the energy consultant.

The tradeoffs: low U vs high VT vs low SHGC

The fundamental tension in Florida commercial glazing specifications

The designer and owner want three things simultaneously from commercial glazing in Florida: good thermal insulation (low U-factor), good solar control (low SHGC), and good daylight (high VT). Physics creates tradeoffs between these objectives that no product can fully eliminate.

Reducing SHGC requires coatings or tints that also reduce VT, because visible light is part of the solar spectrum. Spectrally selective low-e coatings have improved the LSG ratio significantly over the past 15 years — modern coatings achieve SHGC 0.20–0.25 with VT 0.40–0.50, giving LSG ratios of 1.6–2.0. But there is no coating that gives SHGC 0.20 with VT 0.70. The physics do not permit it. When the specification calls for both very low SHGC and high VT, the spec writer is asking for performance that does not exist in commercially certified products — which creates substitution problems at bid.

Similarly, reducing U-factor below 0.30 in a hot Florida climate yields diminishing energy return compared to tightening SHGC. A building energy model will show that in climate zone 2, the annual energy savings from improving SHGC from 0.30 to 0.25 is typically larger than the savings from improving U-factor from 0.35 to 0.30, particularly for buildings with high WWR. Florida specifications that over-specify U-factor at the expense of SHGC optimization are making a thermal performance decision based on cold-climate logic that does not apply to the Florida energy context.

LEED and NFRC ratings for commercial glazing

Where NFRC ratings appear in LEED

LEED v4 and v4.1 reference NFRC ratings in multiple credit categories. The Energy and Atmosphere Optimize Energy Performance credit uses energy modeling with NFRC-certified fenestration values. The Indoor Environmental Quality Daylight and Views credit uses VT directly — to demonstrate an acceptable spatial daylight autonomy calculation, the modeled VT of the glazing must match the NFRC-certified product. Substituting non-NFRC glazing products after the LEED energy model is submitted requires re-running the model with revised values and potentially losing credit points.

For LEED-targeted projects, nail down the glazing product selection and get the NFRC-certified values to the energy modeler before the design energy model is submitted to the LEED review. Changes at construction that affect U-factor, SHGC, or VT require documenting the substitution and updating the LEED submission — a process that takes time and adds risk to the final certification score.

Frequently asked questions

What does NFRC certify?

The National Fenestration Rating Council certifies energy performance ratings for fenestration products: U-factor, SHGC, VT, and AL. NFRC certification is required for products claiming compliant performance values under ASHRAE 90.1 and the Florida Building Code energy provisions. Products without NFRC certification must use default (penalizing) values in energy code compliance calculations.

What U-factor do I need for a Florida commercial project?

Florida falls in ASHRAE climate zones 1, 2, and 3A depending on location. The prescriptive maximum U-factor for fixed commercial glazing under ASHRAE 90.1 varies by climate zone and window-to-wall ratio tier. Confirm the specific maximum U-factor from the current Florida Building Code Energy Conservation volume and the referenced ASHRAE 90.1 edition — these values are updated with each code cycle and vary by WWR. As a practical reference, most Florida commercial glazing specifications target U-0.28 to U-0.40 for dual-pane insulating glass with low-e coating.

What is SHGC in glazing?

SHGC — Solar Heat Gain Coefficient — is the fraction of solar energy that passes through the glazing and enters the building as heat. SHGC ranges from 0.0 to 1.0. Lower SHGC reduces cooling load in Florida's hot climate. Commercial glazing in Florida typically specifies SHGC 0.20–0.35 for facades with significant solar exposure. Confirm the maximum SHGC allowed under the current ASHRAE 90.1 prescriptive table for your climate zone and WWR before specifying.

How is NFRC labeled on a fenestration product?

NFRC-certified products carry a certification label showing U-factor, SHGC, VT, and AL. The NFRC Certified Products Directory at nfrc.org is the authoritative source for current certified values. For commercial projects, always verify the specific product configuration in the NFRC directory — manufacturer data sheets sometimes cite simulation values that differ from certified tested values. Whole-assembly NFRC values are required for energy code compliance; center-of-glass values are not acceptable substitutes.

What is the difference between NFRC certified and AAMA tested?

NFRC certification covers energy performance: U-factor, SHGC, VT, AL. AAMA testing covers structural and water resistance performance: air infiltration, water penetration, structural integrity under wind load, and impact resistance. Both certifications are typically required for Florida commercial glazing products. They are complementary — NFRC confirms energy compliance, AAMA (and NOA for HVHZ) confirm structural and weather resistance compliance. A product can have both certifications.
