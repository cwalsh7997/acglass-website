What is HVHZ — complete guide for commercial projects in Florida

HVHZ shapes every specification decision on commercial glazing in Miami-Dade and Broward. This is the full picture: what the law actually requires, how to verify your project is in the zone, what the testing covers, and what it costs. Written from the field, not the catalog.

By Connor Walsh · President, American Commercial Glass · FAA Pilot · Published May 13, 2026

The short answer

HVHZ — High-Velocity Hurricane Zone — is Miami-Dade and Broward County, Florida, where the Florida Building Code requires Miami-Dade NOA-listed assemblies on every exterior glazing opening. Every frame, glass, anchor, and hardware component must be part of a tested and approved assembly. No NOA, no permit.

Legal definition and geographic boundaries

What the Florida Building Code actually says

The term "High-Velocity Hurricane Zone" is defined in the Florida Building Code (FBC). Miami-Dade and Broward counties constitute the HVHZ. The designation reflects the historical hurricane exposure, wind speed mapping, and local testing infrastructure that these counties developed over decades — most notably after Hurricane Andrew in 1992, which exposed catastrophic failures in low-rise construction and led directly to the current NOA system.

The statutory authority is the Florida Building Code, which adopts the Miami-Dade Product Control Division's NOA system as the benchmark for HVHZ fenestration. When the FBC is updated, HVHZ requirements are updated with it. Always confirm the current edition with the AHJ before specifying — the 8th Edition is the current base code as of 2026, but local amendments may apply.

The boundary question: is your project inside?

The HVHZ boundary is county-based, not a geographic radius or a wind speed contour. If the project parcel is in Miami-Dade or Broward County, it is in HVHZ. No exceptions for inland locations within those counties. A project three miles from the coast in Miramar (Broward) carries the same HVHZ requirements as a project on Brickell Avenue.

Palm Beach County is not in HVHZ by statute. However, some Palm Beach County municipalities — particularly those in higher-risk coastal zones — have adopted local amendments that require NOA-listed assemblies. Always verify with the local building department on Palm Beach projects that are close to the coast or in high-exposure zones. We confirm this on every bid we submit in the county.

Outside HVHZ, Florida uses the Florida Product Approval (FPA) system administered through the Florida Building Commission at floridabuilding.org. FPA is similar in concept but less stringent in testing protocol. See our guide on Florida Product Approval vs. NOA for a detailed comparison.

NOA testing requirements — what the assemblies must survive

The three test protocols

Every fenestration product seeking Miami-Dade NOA approval for HVHZ must pass three core tests under the Test Application Standards (TAS) developed by Miami-Dade Product Control. These tests are not self-certification — they are conducted by accredited testing laboratories under direct witnessing protocols.

TestStandardWhat it coversPass/fail criteria
Large Missile ImpactTAS 2019-lb 2×4 fired at specified velocity at openings ≤30 ft above gradeNo penetration; no opening in glazing; frame must remain attached
Small Missile ImpactTAS 202Steel balls and gravel fired at openings >30 ft above gradeNo penetration; performance sustained through cyclic loading
Cyclic Wind PressureTAS 2039,000 positive and negative pressure cycles simulating hurricane wind loadingNo failure, permanent deformation, or glass displacement during or after cycling

Water infiltration testing (TAS 202.1 / ASTM E331) is also conducted as part of the NOA approval sequence. The assembly must resist water infiltration at specified pressures. The entire system — frame profile, glass type, thickness, interlayer, anchorage pattern, and sealant — is tested together. Substituting any component after approval requires re-testing or an engineer-of-record certification of equivalency.

The cyclic pressure requirement explained

9,000 cycles sounds like a large number because it is. The test simulates the fluctuating positive and negative pressure a building envelope experiences over the duration of a hurricane passing — not a single peak load, but thousands of load reversals. This is why assemblies that handle static design pressure can fail cyclic testing: the glass, spacers, and gaskets fatigue differently under cyclic loads than under a single sustained load.

This is also why ASTM E1300 glass thickness calculations are not sufficient by themselves for HVHZ compliance. E1300 addresses structural capacity under design loads; it does not certify impact resistance or cyclic performance. An HVHZ assembly must have an NOA — period. For more on glass structural design, see our page on ASTM E1300 explained.

AAMA 502 field testing

Laboratory NOA testing certifies the assembly in factory conditions. AAMA 502 is the field test — conducted on the installed, in-place assembly after installation is complete. On most HVHZ commercial projects, the AHJ will require AAMA 502 water testing on a percentage of installed units before issuing a certificate of occupancy. For large curtainwall projects, mock-up testing is required before full installation begins.

Design pressure ranges in HVHZ

Design pressure (DP) is the PSF rating — positive and negative — that an assembly must resist. In HVHZ, DPs are higher than the rest of Florida because the design wind speeds and exposure categories are more severe. The following ranges reflect typical commercial glazing projects; actual values must be calculated by the engineer of record per ASCE 7 and the Florida Building Code.

Building height / locationTypical positive DPTypical negative DPNotes
Low-rise, inland HVHZ (<30 ft)+85 PSF–95 PSFExposure B or C, see ASCE 7 for current values
Mid-rise HVHZ (30–60 ft)+100 PSF–115 PSFCoastal exposure increases negative DP significantly
High-rise HVHZ (60–120 ft)+115 PSF–130 PSFCorner zones exceed mid-zone by 20–30%
Miami coastal high-rise (120+ ft)+130 PSF–145 PSFEOR calculation required; NOA must match or exceed project DP

The negative DP is almost always higher in magnitude than the positive DP. This reflects the suction force of wind on the leeward face and corners of the building. Corner and edge zones per ASCE 7 Chapter 27 carry significantly higher loads than interior zones — this directly affects glass sizing and framing selection. For a full breakdown of DP calculations, see our page on design pressure explained.

What AHJs do and what they check

The role of the Authority Having Jurisdiction

In HVHZ, the AHJ for commercial glazing is typically the county building department (Miami-Dade Building and Neighborhood Services or Broward County Building Division) or the municipality if the project is in an incorporated city. Miami-Dade's Product Control Division maintains the NOA database at miamidade.gov/building and is the issuing authority for all Miami-Dade NOAs.

What the inspector checks at rough-in and closeout

During permit review, the AHJ verifies that the submitted shop drawings and product cut sheets reference valid NOAs with expiration dates in the future and DP ratings that meet or exceed the project's required DPs. At rough-in, inspectors check anchorage spacing, installation method, and that the product label matches the NOA. At closeout, they may require the AAMA 502 field test report, the installer certification, and signed/sealed engineering if deviations were made from the NOA installation instructions.

A common point of failure: the NOA covers one frame depth, but the installer substituted a different depth from the same manufacturer. Even if the products are similar, the combination is no longer NOA-listed. This type of deviation requires the EOR to sign off, which adds time and potential cost.

How to verify your project is HVHZ-compliant before submitting for permit

Step 1 — Confirm HVHZ status with the local building department

Do not rely on GIS maps or third-party tools to make the final determination. Call or query the local building department's permit portal and confirm that the project address is governed by HVHZ requirements and the current Florida Building Code edition.

Step 2 — Verify each product has a current, valid NOA

Search miamidade.gov/building for each product. Verify the NOA number, current expiration date, and that the specific frame depth, glass type, and glass thickness you are specifying are explicitly listed in the NOA. Products covered by the NOA will list the tested configurations — only those exact configurations are approved. See our full breakdown on how to read and verify an NOA.

Step 3 — Verify the assembly DP matches or exceeds the project DP

The NOA specifies the design pressures the tested assembly was rated to. Your project EOR calculates the required DP. The NOA's rated DP must equal or exceed the project's required DP in both positive and negative directions. If the NOA doesn't cover your project DP, you need a different product or an EOR-certified analysis.

Step 4 — Confirm the contractor is qualified

Florida statute requires a licensed contractor for commercial glazing above specified thresholds. In HVHZ, the contractor must also be familiar with NOA installation requirements, AAMA 502 testing procedures, and Miami-Dade Product Control inspection protocols. We hold FL CGC #1531993 and have completed HVHZ installations on commercial projects including Brickell, Coral Gables, Aventura, and Fort Lauderdale properties.

Step 5 — Plan for AAMA 502 testing in the schedule

Field testing requires a separate test procedure with a certified testing firm. Plan 2–3 weeks of float in the closeout schedule for AAMA 502 coordination. Failing a field test does not mean removing all the glass — it means identifying the failure point, correcting the installation, and re-testing. Most failures are at perimeter sealant joints or weep hole covers, not at the glass or frame itself.

Cost premium and what actually drives it

Understanding the cost structure helps owners and GCs make informed decisions about value engineering requests — specifically, it shows which cuts are safe and which compromise the NOA.

Field testing (AAMA 502)1–2%By scope

The premium is not optional. Substituting a non-NOA product to reduce cost means the project cannot get a permit in HVHZ. Value engineering in HVHZ happens by optimizing the NOA configuration — for example, selecting a glass thickness that meets DP without exceeding it, or choosing a frame series that has multiple configurations within a single NOA to cover the full project scope without needing multiple submittals.

Common compliance mistakes we see on HVHZ projects

Specifying FPA products for an HVHZ project

This happens when a spec writer uses a product they know well from non-HVHZ work without confirming the HVHZ-specific NOA. Florida Product Approval and Miami-Dade NOA are different systems. A product with an FPA number and no Miami-Dade NOA cannot be used in HVHZ exterior openings. Check miamidade.gov, not floridabuilding.org, for HVHZ products.

Mixing components from different NOAs

An NOA is tested as a complete assembly. If you use Frame A from NOA-1 and Glass B from NOA-2, neither NOA covers the combination. The assembly is not approved. This is the most common error we see on drawings from out-of-state design teams who are not familiar with the Florida NOA system.

Using an expired NOA

Miami-Dade NOAs expire on a rolling basis. An NOA that was valid when the spec was written may expire before the permit is issued. Always pull the NOA status from miamidade.gov at the time of permit submission, not at the time of spec writing. A project that goes to permit 18 months after design development may have stale NOA references in the spec.

Treating all floors of a building the same

ASCE 7 wind load calculations produce different DPs at different heights. The upper floors and corner zones of a building will have higher DPs than the lower floors and interior zones. Specifying a single NOA assembly for all glazing on a high-rise without confirming that the NOA covers the highest project DP is a documentation error that will surface during permit review.

Overlooking the AAMA 502 requirement

AAMA 502 field testing is not optional on projects where the AHJ requires it. We have seen projects hold up certificate of occupancy because the GC's schedule did not include field testing time. Ask the building department at permit application whether AAMA 502 testing is required. Build it into the schedule and the budget.

Frequently asked questions

What is HVHZ?

HVHZ — High-Velocity Hurricane Zone — is Miami-Dade and Broward County, Florida, where the Florida Building Code requires the highest level of hurricane resistance for all exterior fenestration. Every assembly must hold a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA) proving it passed Large Missile Impact testing (TAS 201), cyclic pressure testing (TAS 203), and water infiltration testing.

Is my project in HVHZ?

If the project is in Miami-Dade or Broward County, it is in HVHZ. The boundary is county-wide with no exceptions for inland location. Palm Beach County is not in HVHZ by statute, but some Palm Beach municipalities have adopted NOA-equivalent requirements through local amendments. Confirm with the local building department before specifying.

Can I use non-HVHZ products inside HVHZ?

No. Products with only Florida Product Approval (FPA) and no Miami-Dade NOA cannot be installed in exterior openings in HVHZ. The AHJ will not approve the permit. Fully interior assemblies that are not exterior openings are a different analysis — confirm with the AHJ and the engineer of record on a case-by-case basis.

What is Large Missile Impact testing?

Large Missile Impact (LMI) testing uses a 9-pound 2×4 timber projectile fired at the assembly at velocities specified in TAS 201. The assembly must resist penetration and then survive 9,000 cyclic pressure cycles (TAS 203) without failure. LMI is required for all fenestration within 30 feet of grade in HVHZ. Small Missile Impact (TAS 202) applies above 30 feet.

What design pressures are typical in HVHZ?

Low-rise commercial glazing in HVHZ typically sees +85/–95 PSF. Mid-rise runs +100/–115 PSF. High-rise runs +115/–130 PSF and higher at corner zones. Exact values depend on building height, exposure category, and location — the engineer of record must calculate project-specific DPs per ASCE 7. See ASCE 7 and the Florida Building Code for current values.

What is AAMA 502 field testing?

AAMA 502 is the field water-resistance test for installed assemblies. On HVHZ commercial projects, AHJs often require AAMA 502 testing on a percentage of installed units before certificate of occupancy. Most failures are at perimeter sealant joints, not at the glass or frame.
