ASTM E1300 explained — glass strength, design loads, and what spec writers miss

ASTM E1300 is the standard every commercial glazing specification references and very few people fully understand. It is not a performance certification — it is a design tool. This page covers what the standard actually tells you, how engineers use it to size glass, where the most common errors occur on commercial specs, and why E1300 alone does not satisfy HVHZ requirements.

By Connor Walsh · President, American Commercial Glass · FL CGC #1531993 · FAA Pilot · Published May 13, 2026

The short answer

ASTM E1300 is the standard practice for determining load resistance of glass in buildings. It tells you the minimum glass thickness and type required to withstand a given design pressure across given panel dimensions. It is a structural sizing tool — it does not certify impact resistance, cyclic performance, or NOA compliance. In HVHZ, E1300 calculations are necessary but not sufficient: the glass must also appear in an NOA-approved glass list.

What ASTM E1300 covers

The scope of the standard

ASTM E1300, Standard Practice for Determining Load Resistance of Glass in Buildings, provides tables and charts that structural engineers use to determine the minimum glass type and nominal thickness for a given set of conditions: the design load (expressed in PSF), the panel dimensions (width by height), and the glass type. The standard covers monolithic glass (annealed, heat-strengthened, fully tempered), laminated glass, and insulating glass units (IGUs) composed of various glass type combinations.

The standard is probabilistic. It is based on an acceptable probability of breakage — the default criterion is that no more than 8 lites in 1,000 (0.8%) will break at the specified design load. The load resistance charts in E1300 reflect that criterion. If a project requires a lower probability of breakage, adjustments are needed. The standard provides guidance for alternate design criteria.

What E1300 does not cover

ASTM E1300 does not certify impact resistance, blast resistance, or cyclic pressure performance. It does not test assemblies — it provides a design methodology for specifying glass. It does not tell you what frame to use, what anchorage pattern to use, or how the glass is retained. E1300 is the starting point for glass specification, not the complete picture. For commercial glazing in Florida, the framing system, anchorage, and combined assembly performance are governed by the product approval system (FPA or NOA) independent of E1300.

Glass type strength factors

The four primary glass types covered by ASTM E1300 have different load resistance capacities. The standard expresses these differences through tables at different probability-of-breakage levels. The following represents the relative strength relationships established in the standard — for specific values, consult the current ASTM E1300 edition published by ASTM International:

Glass typeRelative strength vs annealedFailure modeCommon HVHZ use
Annealed (AN)BaselineLarge, irregular shardsNot approved for HVHZ exterior openings without laminating
Heat-strengthened (HS)Approximately 2× annealedLarger pieces than FT; less hazardous than ANUsed as ply in laminated assemblies for HVHZ
Fully tempered (FT)Approximately 4× annealedSmall, relatively safe fragmentsNot approved as single-ply exterior in HVHZ; used in laminated configurations
Laminated (LG)Depends on ply composition and interlayerFractures but remains in frame if interlayer intactRequired for HVHZ exterior; interlayer holds glass after impact

The strength advantage of fully tempered glass over annealed is significant for structural sizing, but FT alone is not approved for HVHZ exterior openings because upon fracture, FT glass falls out of the frame — even in small fragments, this creates a breach in the building envelope. HVHZ requires laminated glass so that even after fracture, the interlayer holds the glass in place and maintains the envelope. This is why HVHZ glass specs always call for laminated glass with a PVB or ionoplast interlayer, typically using HS or FT plies.

How engineers use ASTM E1300

The basic process

An engineer sizing glass for a commercial project starts with the design pressure — determined from the wind load calculation per ASCE 7. They know the panel dimensions from the architectural drawings. Using the E1300 load resistance charts, they find the minimum glass type and thickness that provides adequate load resistance at the specified DP with acceptable probability of breakage.

For a simple monolithic pane, the process is direct: enter the chart with panel area and design load, read the required thickness for the glass type. For laminated glass, E1300 provides procedures for combining the contributions of each ply, taking into account that a laminated unit's strength is not the simple sum of its plies — the interlayer shear properties affect load sharing between plies.

How it interacts with the framing spec

Glass sizing from E1300 must coordinate with the framing system. The frame must be capable of accepting the glass edge bite required for the thickness, the structural deflection of the frame under load must not overstress the glass bite, and the glass bite itself must be sufficient to retain the glass at design load. These interactions mean that glass sizing is not done independently of framing — the glazing contractor and the EOR coordinate glass specification against the approved framing system's structural data. See our related page on design pressure explained for how DP, deflection limits, and framing interact.

Load duration factor explained

Glass strength is not constant over time — it degrades under sustained load due to stress corrosion in the presence of moisture. ASTM E1300 accounts for this through the load duration factor, which adjusts the apparent strength of glass depending on how long the load is applied. For wind loads (3-second gust), the load duration factor is favorable because wind loads are short in duration. For snow loads, the factor reflects the potential for weeks or months of sustained load and the apparent strength is lower.

In Florida, snow loads are not a design consideration. However, on projects with structural glass floors, glass stairs, overhead glazing under pedestrian traffic, or architectural glass elements under sustained dead load, the load duration factor must be applied correctly. Using the wind load duration factor for a sustained dead load application is a specification error. It results in glass that is structurally undersized for long-term load carrying and may fail progressively over time.

The laminated glass error — treating it as monolithic

The most consequential E1300 error we see on commercial specs is treating a laminated glass unit as if it were a monolithic pane of the same total thickness. A laminated unit composed of two 1/4" plies is not structurally equivalent to a monolithic 1/2" pane under load. The interlayer shear properties determine how load is distributed between the plies, and those properties are temperature and time dependent.

ASTM E1300 provides specific procedures for laminated glass load resistance that account for interlayer shear coupling. For stiff interlayers (ionoplast, such as SentryGlas), the coupling factor approaches 1.0 and the unit behaves closer to monolithic. For softer interlayers (PVB), coupling is lower — particularly at elevated temperatures, which is relevant for Florida glazing exposed to summer solar loading. A PVB-laminate that was sized for structural adequacy at 70°F may behave differently on a south-facing wall that reaches 140°F on a July afternoon. This is not a theoretical concern — it is a real design variable that the EOR must address.

Relationship to HVHZ and NOA compliance

In HVHZ, E1300 and the NOA system address different but overlapping requirements. E1300 determines structural adequacy under load — the glass has sufficient strength to resist the design pressure without exceeding the probability-of-breakage criterion. The NOA addresses impact resistance and cyclic performance — the glass has been physically tested, as part of a specific assembly, to survive a large missile impact and 9,000 cyclic pressure cycles.

These are not the same requirement. A glass configuration that is structurally adequate by E1300 for a given DP may not be the same configuration tested and approved in the NOA. In HVHZ, the NOA's approved glass list controls. If the EOR's E1300 calculation calls for 3/16" + 0.060" PVB + 3/16" laminate, that specific configuration must appear in the NOA's glass list. If the NOA only covers 1/4" + 0.060" PVB + 1/4", you have a mismatch. The solution is to either select a product with an NOA covering the E1300-required configuration, or work with the EOR and manufacturer to verify engineering equivalency — which the AHJ will need to approve.

We see this mismatch most often on curtainwall projects where the structural engineer sizes glass independently from the product selection, then the glazing contractor discovers the NOA doesn't cover the sized configuration. Resolve this during design, not at submittal. For related reading on HVHZ compliance requirements, see our complete guide.

ASTM E1300 vs ASTM E2190

These two standards are often cited together in glazing specs but address completely different aspects of glass performance:

StandardScopeWhat it measuresWho references it
ASTM E1300Structural load resistance of glassGlass thickness and type required for given design load and panel dimensionsStructural engineer, glazing contractor, spec writer
ASTM E2190Insulating glass unit (IGU) performanceEdge seal durability under thermal cycling, humidity exposure, and fogging resistanceSpec writer, owner's rep, energy consultant
ASTM E1886/E1996Impact and cyclic resistanceMissile impact resistance and post-impact cyclic pressure performance for FPAStructural engineer, code compliance, product approval
TAS 201/203HVHZ-specific impact and cyclic testingMiami-Dade NOA certification testing for HVHZ assembliesMiami-Dade Product Control, HVHZ project EOR

ASTM E2190 matters for Florida commercial projects because IGU edge seal performance in high-humidity, high-temperature environments is critical for long-term glass clarity and thermal performance. An IGU that fails its edge seal in year 3 will fog internally and require full replacement. Specifying IGU performance requirements per ASTM E2190 is standard practice on commercial glazing specs and should not be omitted.

What spec writers most commonly miss on E1300

Citing the standard without specifying the glass type

We frequently see specs that say "glass per ASTM E1300" with no specification of glass type (AN, HS, FT, or LG) and no indication of which load resistance chart was used. This leaves the contractor free to interpret, and interpretations vary. Always specify the glass type, the minimum nominal thickness, and the design pressure used for sizing.

Not differentiating zones

ASCE 7 wind load analysis produces different DPs for different zones on a building — interior zones, edge zones, and corner zones. Corner zones can be 1.5 to 2 times the interior zone DP. A spec that specifies a single glass type and thickness for an entire building without differentiating zones may be adequate for interior zones but undersized for corner zones. The EOR must zone the glass specification to match the wind load zoning.

Using nominal thickness as actual thickness

Glass is manufactured to nominal thicknesses with allowed tolerances. "1/4-inch glass" is nominal; the actual thickness may vary. ASTM E1300 uses minimum thickness in its load resistance tables, which is less than nominal. When using E1300, confirm whether the calculation is based on minimum thickness (conservative) or nominal (potentially non-conservative). For critical applications, specify minimum thickness.

Overlooking post-breakage performance

ASTM E1300's probability-of-breakage criterion addresses the probability that glass breaks — not what happens after it breaks. For overhead glazing, glass floors, and applications where broken glass poses safety risk, post-breakage performance (the laminate holding the fragments) must be specified separately. ASTM E1300 Appendix X9 provides guidance on post-breakage structural capacity for laminated glass, but it requires specific analysis — it is not automatic from the standard E1300 procedure.

Frequently asked questions

What does ASTM E1300 cover?

ASTM E1300 is the Standard Practice for Determining Load Resistance of Glass in Buildings. It provides the methodology — charts, tables, and equations — that engineers use to specify minimum glass type and thickness for a given design load and panel dimensions. It covers annealed, heat-strengthened, fully tempered, laminated, and insulating glass unit types.

How is glass thickness calculated?

Using the ASTM E1300 load resistance charts: enter the design pressure (PSF), the panel dimensions (width by height), and the glass type. The chart returns the maximum area a given thickness will support at that load with acceptable probability of breakage (8 per 1,000 default). If the panel area exceeds the value for a given thickness, increase thickness or glass type. For current chart values, consult the current ASTM E1300 edition from ASTM International.

What is load duration factor?

Load duration factor accounts for glass strength degradation under sustained loads. Short-duration wind loads use a favorable factor; long-duration loads (snow, dead load) use a lower factor reflecting reduced apparent strength under sustained stress. Using the wrong load duration factor for a sustained load application — such as a glass floor or overhead glazing — is a common error resulting in undersized glass.

Why does ASTM E1300 matter for HVHZ?

E1300 determines structural glass sizing — but it does not certify impact resistance or NOA compliance in HVHZ. In HVHZ, the glass must also be part of an NOA-listed assembly with its specific type and thickness appearing in the NOA's approved glass list. An E1300-adequate glass configuration that is not in the NOA glass list cannot be used without EOR certification and AHJ review.

What is the difference between ASTM E1300 and ASTM E2190?

ASTM E1300 governs structural sizing of glass under loads. ASTM E2190 governs insulating glass unit (IGU) performance — edge seal durability, thermal cycling resistance, and fogging resistance. Both are referenced in commercial glazing specs and address entirely different performance aspects. E1300 is about structural strength; E2190 is about IGU long-term durability.
