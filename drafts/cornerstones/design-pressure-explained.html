Design pressure (DP) explained for commercial glazing — what spec writers and GCs need to know

Design pressure drives nearly every product selection decision on a commercial glazing project. Get it wrong on the spec and the assembly fails inspection. Ignore the negative DP and the glass may blow out on the leeward side. This page covers what DP is, where it comes from, how it varies by region and height, and how to verify your assembly covers it.

By Connor Walsh · President, American Commercial Glass · FAA Pilot · Published May 13, 2026

The short answer

Design pressure is the positive and negative PSF wind load rating that a glazing assembly must resist. Positive DP is inward wind push; negative DP is outward suction — typically higher in magnitude. DP is calculated per ASCE 7 from wind speed, building height, and exposure category. The assembly's rated DP (from its NOA or FPA) must equal or exceed the project's required DP in both directions.

What design pressure is

The unit and what it represents

Design pressure is expressed in PSF — pounds per square foot — and represents the load per unit area that wind exerts on the building envelope. It has two components: positive (inward) and negative (outward/suction). A typical notation on a project's structural documents looks like "+85/–95 PSF" — meaning the assembly must resist 85 PSF of inward pressure and 95 PSF of outward suction.

The glazing assembly — frame, glass, sealant, and anchorage — must meet or exceed both values. The frame's rated DP from its NOA or FPA governs the maximum allowable DP for the assembly. The glass sizing per ASTM E1300 must be confirmed separately for both positive and negative DP. For HVHZ projects, both the frame and glass must be part of an NOA-listed assembly rated at or above the project DP. See our ASTM E1300 page for a full explanation of how glass is sized under DP loading.

DP is a component and cladding load — not a main wind force

In structural engineering, wind loads are separated into main wind force-resisting system (MWFRS) loads and component and cladding (C&C) loads. MWFRS loads govern the building's primary structure — columns, beams, lateral systems. C&C loads govern individual components like windows, glass panels, and cladding attachments. Design pressure for glazing is a C&C load. The C&C loads from ASCE 7 are often significantly higher than the MWFRS loads, especially in corner and edge zones — which is why specifying glazing based on MWFRS pressures is a common and consequential error.

How design pressure is calculated per ASCE 7

The calculation path

Design pressure is not looked up from a table — it is calculated by the structural engineer through a series of factors applied to the design wind speed. The calculation follows ASCE 7 Chapter 30 for C&C loads on enclosed and partially enclosed buildings. The path:

  1. Design wind speed (V): From the ASCE 7 wind speed maps for the applicable risk category. Florida coastal areas have higher design wind speeds than inland areas. The Florida Building Code adopts ASCE 7 wind speed maps with specific modifications for Florida — confirm the current edition with the EOR.
  2. Velocity pressure (q): Calculated from wind speed: q = 0.00256 × Kz × Kzt × Kd × V². See ASCE 7 for current formula and factor definitions.
  3. Exposure category: A, B, C, or D based on surrounding terrain. Open water or flat open country near a water body is Exposure D (highest loads). Urban and suburban areas are Exposure B (lower loads). Most Florida coastal commercial projects are Exposure C or D.
  4. Height factor (Kz): Velocity pressure increases with height above grade. ASCE 7 Table 26.10-1 provides Kz values by exposure and height — verify from current ASCE 7 edition.
  5. External pressure coefficient (GCp): Zone-specific coefficients from ASCE 7 Figure 30.3-1 (for walls) and 30.3-2 (for roofs). Corner zones have higher negative GCp values than interior zones.
  6. Design pressure: p = q × GCp – qi × GCpi, where qi is the internal pressure and GCpi is the internal pressure coefficient based on enclosure classification.

As a contractor, my job starts where this calculation ends — I take the EOR's DP output and verify that every product I am proposing has NOA/FPA coverage at or above the required DP. I have had projects where the EOR's DP exceeded the available NOA-listed product capacity in a specific configuration — that becomes a coordination issue that must be resolved before permit submission, not after.

Positive vs negative DP — why negative is higher

Negative design pressures are almost always higher in magnitude than positive pressures on the same building face. This is counterintuitive to some GCs and spec writers who expect the "push" of wind to govern. It does not. Here is why:

On the windward face of a building, positive wind pressure pushes the wall in. The positive pressure coefficient for a typical wall interior zone is approximately 0.8 (from ASCE 7 — see current edition for exact values). But on the leeward face, and especially at corner and edge zones, wind acceleration around the building corners creates strong suction zones. The negative pressure coefficient for corner zones can be –1.8 or higher. Combined with internal positive pressure from an enclosed building, the outward net pressure on a corner panel can be substantially higher than the inward pressure on a windward interior panel.

This is why a project spec might show +85/–110 PSF — the 110 PSF negative governs the glass sizing and the frame design, not the 85 PSF positive. Both values must be checked against the assembly rating, and both must be verified against the NOA or FPA. The frame deflection under negative DP is also a critical check — the frame must not bow outward past L/175 (storefront) or L/240 (curtainwall) under peak negative load, because excessive deflection compromises the glass bite and the water seal at the perimeter.

Deflection limits — L/175 and L/240

Why deflection limits matter

Design pressure determines the load. Deflection limits determine whether the framing can carry that load without bowing enough to damage the glass, break the water seal, or look unacceptable. The deflection limit is expressed as a fraction of the member span — L/175 or L/240 — where L is the unsupported length of the member in inches. At L/175, a 10-foot (120") member can deflect a maximum of 120/175 = 0.69". At L/240, the same member can deflect only 120/240 = 0.50".

Storefront vs curtainwall deflection limits

The industry standard deflection limit for storefront framing is L/175 per AAMA 501 and AAMA 2501. Curtainwall framing is typically held to L/240 or L/175 depending on the glass type and joint design — L/240 is the more common curtainwall standard. Some projects and some framing systems have more restrictive limits specified by the EOR. The framing manufacturer's span charts are published for specific deflection limits and load cases — when you size a storefront frame from a span chart, the chart implicitly uses L/175. If your project spec requires L/240, you are working with a different chart and the allowable spans will be shorter.

When a storefront frame is pushed toward its capacity — say, a 9-ft storefront in HVHZ at +85/–95 PSF — the framing manufacturer's span chart will show whether the specified frame series can meet L/175 at that span and load combination. If it cannot, you have three options: increase frame depth (if the system offers a deeper series), reduce the span with intermediate horizontal members, or switch to a curtainwall system with deeper framing. Trying to install a frame that does not meet its deflection limit is a compliance failure that the inspector will catch. Building zones and corner loads

ASCE 7 zoning — why not all glass on a building is the same

ASCE 7 divides the building envelope into zones based on wind pressure intensity. Interior zones experience lower wind pressures than edge zones, which in turn are lower than corner zones. For a typical low-rise building, the corner zone can have C&C negative pressure coefficients roughly twice the interior zone coefficient. This means the glazing at building corners may need a higher DP-rated assembly than the glazing at the same height on the interior face.

The common error is writing a single-line glass spec — "1/4" tempered" or "6mm laminated per E1300 at +85/–95 PSF" — applied uniformly across all building elevations without zone differentiation. The EOR's wind load calculations will show different DP requirements by zone. The spec must track those zones with zone-specific glass specs. On large commercial projects, we request the EOR's wind load zone map during design development so we can confirm that our product selections cover all zones before the spec is finalized.

How to verify an assembly meets the project DP

Step 1 — Get the project's required DPs from the EOR

The project EOR issues a wind load summary that identifies the required C&C DP for each zone of the building. If you do not have this document, request it before specifying any product. Contractors who bid from preliminary drawings without a confirmed wind load summary are exposing themselves to change order risk when the EOR's final DPs are released.

Step 2 — Pull the NOA or FPA for the specified product

From the NOA or FPA, find the rated DP for the specific frame size and glass configuration you are specifying. The rated DP must equal or exceed the project's required DP in both positive and negative directions. If the NOA rates the assembly at +85/–95 PSF and the project requires +85/–110 PSF, the assembly does not cover the project — full stop. You need a product with an NOA or FPA covering +85/–110 PSF or higher.

Step 3 — Verify maximum unit dimensions

The NOA/FPA specifies the maximum unit width and height for the tested assembly. If the project has units larger than the approved maximum, the assembly is not covered — even if the DP ratings match. Confirm maximum unit dimensions against the actual unit schedule before submitting for permit.

Step 4 — Verify deflection compliance

Using the framing manufacturer's published span charts, confirm that the specified frame at the project's DP and span meets the required deflection limit (L/175 for storefront, L/240 for curtainwall). If it does not, increase the frame depth, add intermediate supports, or switch systems. Document the span chart reference in the submittal package.

Common DP errors on commercial glazing specs

Using MWFRS pressures instead of C&C pressures

Main wind force-resisting system loads are lower than component and cladding loads. Using MWFRS pressures for glazing sizing underestimates the actual DP by 20–40%, especially at corners and edges. Glazing is a C&C component — always use C&C pressures from ASCE 7 Chapter 30 for fenestration sizing.

Not specifying both positive and negative DP

A spec that says "design pressure: 85 PSF" without indicating whether that is positive, negative, or both is incomplete. Both values must be specified and both must be checked against the assembly rating. The negative value almost always governs glass sizing on coastal Florida projects.

Specifying DP without referencing the product approval

Writing "+85/–95 PSF" in the spec without referencing the NOA or FPA number and the specific approved configuration means the DP requirement is stated but not verified. When we review drawings as part of a bid, we pull every NOA/FPA for every product referenced and confirm the rated DP matches. We find mismatches frequently on out-of-state design teams' Florida drawings.

Applying corner zone DP uniformly

Corner zone DPs can be 50–100% higher than interior zone DPs. If you specify the corner zone DP for all glazing on the building, you are over-specifying the interior zones, which adds cost. If you specify the interior zone DP for all glazing, you are under-specifying the corners, which creates a code compliance failure. Zone the spec.

Not updating DP when the building height or footprint changes

Wind loads increase with height. A building that grows from 3 stories to 4 stories in a design revision may see upper floor DPs increase meaningfully. If the glazing spec is not updated to reflect the new height, the upper floor glazing may be undersized. On fast-track projects where we are bidding from schematic or design development drawings, we always request confirmation of the wind load calculations before purchasing framing and glass.

Frequently asked questions

What is design pressure in commercial glazing?

Design pressure (DP) is the PSF load rating — positive (inward) and negative (outward suction) — that a glazing assembly must structurally resist. It is the primary structural criterion for selecting commercial glazing systems in Florida and most code jurisdictions. The assembly's rated DP from its NOA or FPA must meet or exceed the project's required DP in both directions.

How is design pressure calculated?

The structural engineer calculates DP from the design wind speed (ASCE 7 wind speed maps), building height, exposure category, directionality factor, topographic factor, and building enclosure classification, following ASCE 7 Chapter 30 for component and cladding loads. The result is zone-specific DPs for each face and zone of the building. See ASCE 7 (current edition) for the complete calculation methodology and current factors.

What is L/175 vs L/240 deflection limit?

L/175 means framing deflection under design load must not exceed the span divided by 175 — for a 10-ft (120") member, that is 0.69" maximum midspan deflection. L/240 is more restrictive: the same 10-ft member can deflect only 0.50". Storefront is typically designed to L/175; curtainwall to L/240. More restrictive deflection limits reduce glass breakage risk and improve water resistance at perimeter seals.

What design pressure do I need in West Palm Beach?

West Palm Beach commercial glazing is non-HVHZ and requires Florida Product Approval (FPA). Typical indicative ranges for low-rise coastal WPB: +60 to +80 PSF positive, –70 to –90 PSF negative. Actual values depend on building height, exposure category, and specific location. The project EOR must calculate project-specific DPs per ASCE 7. These are indicative only — see ASCE 7 and the Florida Building Code for current values.

Why are negative design pressures higher than positive?

Negative pressures are higher because of wind flow around the building. On windward faces, wind pushes with positive pressure coefficients around 0.8. On leeward faces and especially at corners, wind acceleration creates suction zones with negative pressure coefficients that can reach –1.8 or higher — plus internal positive pressure adds to the net outward load. This is why ASCE 7 C&C negative pressures govern glass sizing on most Florida commercial projects.
