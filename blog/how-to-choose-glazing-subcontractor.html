How to Choose a
Glazing Subcontractor

Rielly Walsh · President, ACG

What to look for, what to avoid, and why the GC-glazing sub relationship makes or breaks the glazing scope on complex commercial projects.

Rielly Walsh · 2026-04-10 · 7 min read

Glazing is on the critical path of nearly every commercial project. The storefront can't close until the frame is set. The curtainwall delays the interior finishes behind it. A missed inspection on the impact windows holds the certificate of occupancy. Choosing the right glazing subcontractor is one of the highest-leverage procurement decisions a GC makes - and it's one that often receives less attention than it deserves until something goes wrong.

What to Look for When Evaluating a Glazing Sub

Licensing and Insurance

In Florida, commercial glazing contractors must hold a valid state contractor license. Verify the license is active and carries the correct classification for the work scope - glazing, specialty structures, or general contracting with glazing subcontract authority depending on jurisdiction. Require a current certificate of insurance before any qualification conversation continues. GL coverage minimums for commercial glazing typically run $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate. Workers' compensation is non-negotiable. Confirm the COI names your company as additional insured before contract execution.

Submittal Quality and Speed

Glazing submittals - shop drawings, product data, Florida Product Approval documentation, and PE-stamped calculations where required - sit on the project's critical path. A glazing sub that takes six to eight weeks to produce first submittals is already behind. Ask directly: what is your typical turnaround from contract execution to first complete submittal package? A qualified commercial glazing sub should answer in business days, not weeks. Ask to see a sample submittal package from a recent similar project. Disorganized, incomplete submittals on a similar project predict the same on yours.

References from GCs on Similar Work

References should come from general contractors - not building owners - on projects of comparable type and complexity. A glazing sub with strong references on retail storefront but no track record on a 10-story curtainwall project is not the same sub for both jobs. Ask the reference three specific questions: Did submittals come in on time and complete? Were there any surprise schedule delays tied to the glazing scope? Would you hire them again on a project with the same complexity?

Schedule Performance Track Record

Ask how the sub manages material lead times and communicates schedule risk. Commercial glazing materials - aluminum extrusions, insulated glass units, specialty hardware - routinely run 8-16 week lead times. A glazing sub that orders materials immediately after contract execution and tracks lead times proactively against the project schedule is operationally sound. One that waits for submittal approval before ordering materials will put you in a position where the glazing critical path extends weeks past what the schedule shows.

Dedicated Project Management

On a commercial project, a glazing sub needs a dedicated PM - not just an estimator who wins the work and a field foreman who manages the crew. The PM is the interface between the glazing sub's operations and the GC's superintendent: managing submittals, tracking material deliveries, coordinating inspection requests, responding to RFIs, and escalating when something is at risk. Ask who the PM will be on your project, what their current workload looks like, and whether they'll have backup coverage. A sub that can't name a PM before contract execution is telling you how the project will be managed.

Red Flags During the Qualification and Bid Process

Slow response during the bid phase. If a glazing sub takes three days to answer a scope question during the bid process, that's the communication pace you'll experience during the project. Response time during qualification is a reliable proxy for responsiveness during execution.

No dedicated PM. The estimator who bids the job and the field foreman who runs the crew are not a substitute for a project manager. On any project above a basic retail storefront, the absence of a dedicated PM is a structural problem, not a staffing gap that will resolve itself after award.

Unclear scope inclusions and exclusions. Glazing bids that rely on broad allowances, vague product specs, or extensive exclusion lists create risk. Caulking, door hardware, thresholds, automatic entrance operators, and perimeter flashing are common exclusions that end up as change orders. A qualified sub prices and includes these items explicitly.

References that don't match the project type. Residential and light commercial references don't transfer to complex commercial work. Ask specifically about projects with the same glazing system type (curtainwall, window wall, storefront), similar size, and in Florida if applicable. HVHZ compliance in particular requires demonstrated experience - not just a statement that the sub "does HVHZ work."

Inability to speak to Florida Product Approval specifics. In Florida, every exterior glazing product requires a state product approval number. A glazing sub who can't recite their standard system PA numbers, or who can't explain the difference between a Florida PA and a Miami-Dade NOA, is not a regular commercial player in this state.

Why the GC-Glazing Sub Relationship Matters on Complex Projects

On a simple retail storefront, the tolerance for a marginal glazing sub is higher. The scope is straightforward, the lead times are short, and the inspection process is uncomplicated. On a complex project - a 15-story mixed-use with curtainwall and window wall, a hospital with fire-rated glazing assemblies throughout, a HVHZ building that requires Miami-Dade NOA products - the glazing sub's operational quality becomes a multiplier on project risk.

At that complexity level, the GC's superintendent needs a glazing PM who communicates proactively, not reactively. They need submittals that come in complete and on time, so the structural engineer and architect can review them without the three-week back-and-forth that eats the schedule buffer. They need material orders placed the week after contract execution, not the week the GC starts asking where the submittals are.

The best glazing subs operate as an extension of the GC's project management - not as an independent contractor who shows up when material arrives. Building that relationship starts with qualification, and qualification starts with the questions outlined above.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I look for when qualifying a glazing subcontractor?

The key qualifiers are: a valid Florida glazing contractor license, GL and workers' comp insurance certificates, verifiable references from GCs on similar project types and sizes, a demonstrated submittal process with typical first-submittal turnaround, dedicated project management (not just an estimator and a field crew), and fluency with Florida Product Approval and NOA documentation if you're building in South Florida. Also ask how they track material lead times and communicate schedule risk - vague answers are a red flag.

What are the red flags when reviewing a glazing sub's bid?

Watch for large allowances where firm pricing should exist, scope exclusions that shift risk to the GC (like caulking, hardware, or flashing), vague product specifications that leave substitution room, no dedicated PM identified, and references that are all residential or light commercial. A slow response time during the bid process - more than 48 hours to answer a scope question - often predicts the same behavior during the project.

Why does the GC-glazing sub relationship matter more on complex projects?

On complex projects - high-rise curtainwall, HVHZ-regulated buildings, healthcare facilities, mixed-use with multiple glazing systems - the glazing scope has more interfaces with other trades, longer lead times, more submittal complexity, and higher stakes at inspection. A glazing sub that communicates well, delivers submittals on time, and flags problems early keeps the project moving. One that goes quiet, delivers late submittals, or surprises the GC with missed lead times can add weeks to the critical path.
