Whole-Window vs Center-of-Glass U-Factor:
What Belongs in the Submittal?

Same building. Two numbers. Different documents. A worksheet for specs and bids, not a table of invented U-values.

Quick Answer

Whole-window (total fenestration product) U-factor covers the full assembly per NFRC. Center-of-glass covers the central glazing only. Specs, energy models, and NFRC certificates usually want whole-product. Glass schedules sometimes list COG for makeup. Never mix bases in a bid comparison. This page resolves a reporting mismatch: which U-factor goes in which document. It is not a Florida climate-zone limit table. For limit overviews, use ACG’s energy-code compliance page and then verify current code text for the project.

Two definitions, carefully

Public NFRC procedure language (ANSI/NFRC 100) distinguishes:

  • Total fenestration product U-factor (Ut): the U-factor representative of the total system. In practice this is what people mean by whole-window or whole-product U-factor. It accounts for center of glazing, edge of glazing, frame, dividers where applicable, and related components under the NFRC rating method.
  • Center-of-glazing U-factor (Uc): the U-factor representative of the center-of-glazing area only. NFRC defines the center-of-glazing area as glazing away from the frame/sash/divider edge zone (commonly described as outside a 2.5 in / 63.5 mm band from those edges in NFRC 100 definitions).

NFRC also explains in public education material that labeled U-factor is whole-product performance, not glass alone. Cite the controlling NFRC document edition on the project submittal rather than paraphrasing from memory.

Why the numbers differ

Frames and edge-of-glass regions usually transfer more heat than the center of an insulating glass unit. For the same glass makeup, whole-product U-factor is typically higher (worse) than center-of-glass U-factor. That is normal physics under area-weighted assembly rating, not a vendor error.

This draft intentionally includes no sample U-factor numbers. Pull configuration-specific values from manufacturer NFRC documentation, CMA reports, or certified label certificates for the sizes and frame types on your schedule.

Bid-day mistakes to avoid

  • Comparing a glass-only COG from a coating brochure to a whole-window U-factor limit in the spec.
  • Omitting frame type (thermally broken vs non-thermally broken) when stating whole-product U-factor.
  • Using a residential NFRC size basis for a commercial curtain wall or storefront without checking the commercial rating path (CMA / site-built as applicable).
  • Changing Low-E and recycling old thermal sheets without re-pulling the configuration. See Low-E change vs approved system.

Submittal language that stays clean

Prefer two lines over one vague line:

  • “Whole-product U-factor (NFRC / total fenestration product): [value from certified docs for config X]”
  • “Center-of-glass U-factor: [value from glass maker sheet for makeup Y]”

Leave the brackets empty until documents are in hand. Do not invent fillers for bid day.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is whole-window U-factor?

In NFRC terms, the total fenestration product U-factor (often called whole-window or whole-product U-factor) represents heat transfer for the complete assembly, including center of glazing, edge of glazing, frame, and related components as defined in ANSI/NFRC 100. It is the value used for product comparison on NFRC labels and certificates.

What is center-of-glass U-factor?

Center-of-glass (center-of-glazing) U-factor represents the central glazing area only, excluding frame and edge effects as defined in NFRC procedures. It is useful for glass makeup comparisons. It is not a substitute for whole-product U-factor when the specification or code path asks for fenestration product performance.

Which U-factor belongs in a commercial submittal?

Use the value the project documents require, stated with its basis. If Division 08 or the energy model calls for NFRC whole-product U-factor, do not answer with center-of-glass alone. If a glass schedule asks for center-of-glass for makeup selection, label it COG explicitly.

Why do bids fail when U-factors look better on paper?

A common failure is comparing a center-of-glass brochure number to a whole-window specification limit. COG is usually lower (appears better) than whole-product U-factor for the same makeup. Always state the reporting basis next to the number.
