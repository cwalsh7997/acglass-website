Fire-Rated Glass:
What Architects Need to
Know About Code Compliance

Understanding fire-protective vs. fire-resistive glazing, rating requirements by application, UL listings, and common specification errors that lead to code violations.

Why Fire-Rated Glass Matters

Fire-rated glass protects life and property by preventing flame and hot smoke from passing through openings during fires. Building codes require fire-rated glazing in specific applications—particularly where the glass is part of a fire-rated wall or assembly. Installing non-rated glass where fire-rated glass is required results in code violation, failed inspections, and potential liability if a fire occurs.

Understanding the distinction between fire-protective and fire-resistive glass, identifying where each is required, and ensuring proper installation is essential for architects, contractors, and building officials.

Fire-Protective vs. Fire-Resistive: The Critical Difference

Fire-Protective Glass: Fire-protective glazing prevents passage of flame and smoke through the glass for the rated time period (typically 20 minutes). This is the minimum fire-safety requirement and is achieved through specially treated tempered glass or proprietary glass compositions that resist breakage and prevent breach. Fire-protective glass may allow some heat transmission but prevents flames and smoke from passing through.

Fire-protective glass is used in applications where the primary goal is preventing flame and smoke passage—such as vision panels in fire-rated doors or windows in fire-rated walls separating tenant spaces.

Fire-Resistive Glass: Fire-resistive glazing maintains insulating properties and prevents passage of flame, smoke, AND heat beyond the rated time period (typically 20, 30, or 60 minutes). Fire-resistive glass is more sophisticated, often incorporating ceramic materials or multiple layers. It performs significantly better at limiting heat transmission through the glass assembly.

Fire-resistive glass is required in applications where both fire separation and insulation are critical—such as wall assemblies adjacent to occupied spaces, where uncontrolled heat transmission could ignite materials beyond the wall or harm occupants.

International Building Code Requirements by Application

Fire-Rated Doors: Vision panels (windows) in fire-rated doors require fire-protective glazing rated for the door's fire rating (typically 20 minutes for B-label doors, 45 minutes for A-label doors). The vision panel must be limited in size per code—typically 100 sq. in. maximum for 20-minute doors.

Fire-Rated Corridor and Area Separation Walls: Windows in walls separating buildings or creating exit corridors require fire-resistive glazing matching the wall rating (20, 30, or 60 minutes depending on application). These assemblies require both flame/smoke protection and insulation to prevent fire spread.

Exit Corridors and Exit Access Doors: Vision panels in exit corridor walls must meet the wall's fire rating, typically 1-hour. Fire-resistive glass is typically required because these walls protect egress routes and must prevent fire from entering the corridor from either side.

Tenant Separation (Multifamily and Office): In multifamily buildings, demising walls between units typically require 1-hour fire rating. Any glazing in these walls must be fire-resistive glass rated for 1 hour. This is frequently missed in specifications and leads to code violations on multifamily projects.

Exterior Walls (Depending on Occupancy and Height): Exterior walls in certain occupancies may require fire-resistive glazing based on the building's fire-separation distance from property lines or adjacent buildings.

Understanding UL Listings and Testing

Fire-rated glass products must carry Underwriters Laboratories (UL) listings that document their performance. UL testing simulates fire conditions and measures the product's ability to prevent flame, smoke, and heat passage.

UL 9 Standard: Fire Tests of Building Construction and Materials is the governing standard. Glass products are tested in assemblies with approved frames and installed per manufacturer specifications. The UL listing specifies exactly how the product can be installed and what ratings it achieves.

Critical UL Details: A UL listing is not universal. The same glass product may achieve different ratings when installed in different frames or assemblies. The UL listing number and revision are specific to the exact product, frame, and installation method tested. Installing fire-rated glass in a frame different from the tested assembly voids the rating and creates a code violation.

Verification Process: Always verify that the proposed glass product and frame assembly combination carries a valid UL listing for your specific application and rating requirement. ACG maintains a current database of UL listings and verifies product selections before submittal to ensure code compliance.

Common Specification Errors That Cause Code Violations

Error 1: Specifying Fire-Protective Where Fire-Resistive Is Required. Using fire-protective glass in a multifamily demising wall or exit corridor wall violates code. These applications require fire-resistive glass with insulating properties. Verify the required rating and ensure specifications call for the correct glass type.

Error 2: Incorrectly Sizing Vision Panels. Fire-rated door vision panels have maximum area limits. A door label may permit 100 sq. in. of vision panel; specifying a larger panel violates code and the door assembly. Verify panel size against door label requirements.

Error 3: Installing Rated Glass in Non-Rated Frames. Fire-rated glass must be installed in frames approved for that rating. Installing fire-rated glass in a standard storefront frame voids the rating. The entire assembly must be UL-listed and rated.

Error 4: Using Outdated or Incorrect UL Listings. UL listings are periodically updated or revised. An older listing may be superseded by a new revision with different installation requirements. Always verify the current, applicable UL listing before installation.

Error 5: Not Identifying All Fire-Rated Walls During Design. Multifamily projects often have numerous demising walls requiring fire-rated glazing. During design, clearly identify all fire-rated wall assemblies and ensure all glazing specifications match rating requirements. This prevents late-stage specification changes and field conflicts.

Installation and Inspection Considerations

Fire-rated glass installation requires precision. The frame must be exactly as specified in the UL listing. Gaskets, sealants, and fastening methods must match approved details. Deviations from approved details can compromise the rating.

Building officials inspect fire-rated glazing before walls are closed. They verify that products carry valid UL listings, that installation matches approved details, and that the assembly is complete and correct. Non-compliant installations are not approved and must be corrected or replaced before building closure.

ACG ensures fire-rated installations comply with code by verifying UL listings, following approved details precisely, and coordinating with building inspectors before and during installation.

Practical Specification Guidance

For Architects and Specifiers: Early in design, identify all fire-rated wall assemblies. Require fire-resistant glass in demising walls, area separation walls, exit corridors, and any application where the wall is fire-rated. Specify the required rating (20, 30, or 60 minutes) and the frame assembly by UL listing number. Communicate with the glazing contractor to confirm product availability and lead times.

For Contractors: When you encounter fire-rated glazing in specifications, immediately consult with the glass supplier to confirm product availability and UL listing. Order products early—fire-rated glass often has longer lead times than standard glass. Verify installation details match the UL listing exactly.

For Building Officials: During inspections, verify that fire-rated glazing carries valid UL listings for the stated application. Confirm that installed products match shop drawings and approved details. Do not approve installations that deviate from approved UL details.

Cost and Schedule Impact

Fire-rated glass costs more than standard glass—typically 1.5x to 3x the cost of equivalent standard glazing, depending on the rating and product type. Lead times are also longer, often 10-14 weeks for custom assemblies. These cost and schedule impacts should be factored into project budgets early.

Discovering fire-rated requirements late in design or during construction is costly. Every effort should be made to identify fire-rated glazing requirements during the design phase.
