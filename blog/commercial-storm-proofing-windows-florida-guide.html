Storm proofing a Florida commercial property is more than swapping in impact windows. It is a layered envelope strategy - impact glazing as the primary defense, sealed perimeter details, rated entrances, reinforced overhead doors, pre-season inspections, and a post-storm response plan. This guide walks through what commercial storm proofing actually means under Florida Building Code 2023, what components each layer adds, and how Florida owners, property managers, and GCs build a building envelope that stays sealed through Category 4 and 5 events.

What Storm Proofing a Commercial Building Actually Means

There is no building that is fully "storm proof." What exists is a rated envelope - a combination of glazing, cladding, roofing, entrances, and openings that are each tested and documented to withstand a specified wind pressure and impact load. For Florida commercial work, those ratings are defined by ASTM E1886, ASTM E1996, and the related Miami-Dade TAS 201/202/203 protocols, and the full system is permitted under Florida Building Code (FBC) 8th Edition 2023.

A properly storm-proofed Florida commercial envelope has five elements working together:

  • Impact-rated glazing at every opening below the wind-borne debris threshold height
  • Properly installed perimeter sealant, flashing, and structural anchoring
  • Rated entrances (automatic and manual) with tested pressure and impact performance
  • Reinforced overhead doors, rolling steel, or impact-rated shutters at loading and service openings
  • A roof-to-wall interface and roof system rated for the site design wind speed

Each layer stops a different failure mode. Glazing that holds through a missile strike is irrelevant if the adjacent sealant detail lets wind-driven rain breach the envelope. A sealed envelope is irrelevant if the main entrance blows in and pressurizes the interior. Storm proofing a commercial building is a systems exercise.

Layer 1: Impact-Rated Glazing as the Primary Defense

Glazing is the most extensive surface area in most commercial envelopes and the most exposed to wind-borne debris. Impact-rated commercial glazing is laminated glass (PVB or SGP interlayer) in a reinforced frame, tested to ASTM E1886/E1996 at Missile Level D for commercial applications above the 30-foot height threshold and Missile Level C/D below.

What Impact Rating Actually Certifies

The impact test fires a 9-pound 2x4 projectile at 50 feet per second into the glazing assembly, followed by 9,000 cycles of positive and negative pressure simulating a full hurricane cycle. An assembly that passes is rated for the specified design pressure (DP) and Missile Level and receives a Florida Product Approval (FL number) or Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA) that ties it to specific frame types, glass makeups, and installation details.

Common Florida commercial impact systems ACG installs, with typical ratings:

  • ESWindows ES-8000 pre-glazed storefront - DP +60/-70, Missile Level D, NOA-listed for HVHZ
  • ESWindows ES-9000 window wall - DP +70/-80 depending on module, Missile Level D
  • ESWindows SGD-2020 sliding glass door - commercial impact-rated for HVHZ and non-HVHZ
  • Punched impact commercial window - DP +50/-60 typical, Missile Level D

What Impact Glass Does Not Cover

Impact glazing rates the assembly, not the installation. A rated assembly installed with insufficient anchoring, wrong sealant, or wrong substrate attachment will still fail. Installation documentation - shop drawings, product approval referenced on the drawings, anchor schedules, sealant specifications - is as important as the product itself. Layer 2: Perimeter Sealant, Flashing, and Anchoring

The joint between the glazing system and the building structure is the most common failure point in commercial storm events. Water infiltration, air pressurization, and framing deflection all concentrate at this perimeter. Proper storm proofing treats the perimeter as a three-stage system:

Structural Anchoring

The frame must transfer wind pressure from the glass to the building structure at the spacing and fastener schedule shown on the product approval. Substituted anchors, longer spacing, or unsuitable substrates (unreinforced CMU, hollow core, corroded steel) void the rating. The permit inspection specifically checks anchoring on commercial glazing.

Air and Water Seal

The sealant between frame and substrate - typically a high-movement silicone meeting ASTM C920, Type S or M - carries the air and water barrier from the wall to the frame. Specified joint geometry (minimum bead depth, backer rod size, substrate preparation) is critical. Hurricane water intrusion on commercial properties almost always traces back to compromised or missing perimeter sealant rather than glass failure itself.

Flashing and Head/Sill Detailing

Head flashing and sill pan conditions must drain wind-driven rain back to the exterior. On storefront retrofits, adding a sill pan under a new frame is a common detail even when the original lacked one. This is one of the highest-value details we scope on any storm-proofing retrofit.

Layer 3: Rated Entrances

The main entrance is both the most extensive moving component in the envelope and the most common storm failure on commercial properties. A standard commercial entrance is a narrow-stile aluminum door that is typically not impact-rated. Storm proofing requires substituting or supplementing with an impact-rated entrance solution.

Impact-Rated Automatic Sliding Entrances

Assa Abloy Besam, Horton, and Record USA all produce impact-rated automatic sliding entrance packages that carry Florida Product Approvals. These integrate with the storefront envelope and maintain ADA and egress compliance. For higher-traffic properties - hospitality, medical, large retail - this is the right primary entrance solution.

Impact-Rated Swing Doors

Medium-stile and wide-stile impact-rated swing doors from major commercial door manufacturers meet NOA requirements for HVHZ. These are typical on secondary entrances, back-of-house, and smaller retail storefronts.

Impact-Rated Shutters as a Secondary Layer

On properties where full entrance replacement is not in scope, impact-rated accordion or roll-down shutters over existing non-impact entrances can provide the code-equivalent protection. The tradeoff is operational - shutters must be deployed before the storm, which assumes a staffed property with hurricane protocols. See our impact windows vs shutters article for the full comparison.

Layer 4: Service Openings and Overhead Doors

Loading docks, service bays, warehouse doors, and parking garage openings are often overlooked in storm proofing. A blown-in overhead door pressurizes the building interior and triggers roof uplift - which is how many commercial envelope failures actually start.

Impact-Rated Overhead Doors

Manufacturers including C.H.I., Clopay, and Wayne Dalton produce impact-rated insulated steel overhead doors carrying Florida Product Approvals. These are specified on any new commercial project with loading, service, or vehicle openings, and they are a high-priority retrofit on older facilities.

Rolling Steel and Fire-Rated Assemblies

Rolling steel security doors and rated fire/life-safety rolling assemblies also have impact-rated product lines. On commercial properties with large openings facing parking fields or roadways - prime debris-path orientations - these are not optional.

Layer 5: Roof System and Roof-to-Wall Interface

Glazing and wall storm proofing is wasted capital if the roof fails. Most Florida commercial roof failures start at the roof-to-wall interface, parapet coping, or a compromised penetration detail. A full storm-proofing scope includes inspection (and usually recommissioning) of:

  • Parapet cap and coping anchoring
  • Roof membrane edge termination
  • Mechanical unit curbs and roof penetrations
  • Roof drain and scupper conditions

Envelope trades coordinate with the roofing consultant on the roof-to-wall interface. On a complete envelope storm-proofing project, ACG coordinates the glazing scope with the roofing scope so the interface detail transfers correctly.

Pre-Season and Post-Storm Inspection

Even a properly storm-proofed envelope requires active management. Florida commercial property managers should run two annual inspection passes:

Pre-Season Inspection (May-June)

  • Perimeter sealant condition (gaps, cracks, adhesion failure)
  • Glazing gasket and weather-stripping condition
  • Entrance operator function (automatic and swing)
  • Overhead door operation and seal
  • Roof drain clearance and parapet cap condition
  • Shutter deployment (if part of envelope strategy)
  • Documentation review: Florida Product Approvals, closeout binder, insurance carrier requirements

Post-Storm Inspection

  • Glazing for impact damage or stress cracks
  • Frame deflection or anchor pullout
  • Perimeter sealant for rupture
  • Interior moisture or staining at window heads and sills
  • Entrance and overhead door operation
  • Roof membrane and parapet integrity
  • Insurance claim documentation for any damage found

Insurance and Documentation

Florida commercial insurance carriers recognize storm-proofed envelopes. Documentation that supports wind mitigation credits and streamlines claim handling includes Florida Product Approvals, Notice of Acceptance documents, permit closeouts, shop drawings, anchor schedules, sealant warranties, and photo documentation. A complete closeout binder - which ACG delivers as standard on every project - is the artifact that lets insurance carriers price risk correctly. Common Storm-Proofing Mistakes on Florida Commercial Projects

Three patterns consistently compromise storm-proofing outcomes:

  1. Spot retrofits without the system: Replacing storefront glazing while leaving non-impact entrance doors and overhead doors in place. The weakest opening defines the envelope.
  2. Skipping perimeter recommissioning: Installing new impact glazing over existing compromised sealant. The glass holds; the perimeter leaks.
  3. Using residential-rated products on commercial projects: Not every impact window carries commercial DP ratings. Mid-rise and large-footprint commercial requires commercial-class products - typically ESWindows ES-8000/9000 or equivalent - with commercial-scale NOAs.
