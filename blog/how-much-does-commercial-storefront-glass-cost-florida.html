Why Commercial Glass Pricing Is Hard to Pin Down

The honest answer is that "commercial glass" covers an enormous range of products and systems - from a simple tempered pane in a hollow metal frame to a structural silicone curtainwall with triple-silver Low-E coating on a 30-story tower. Those two things are both "commercial glass," but their costs are an order of magnitude apart.

The number that matters to your budget is the installed cost per square foot - meaning the glass, the framing system, all hardware, shop drawings, fabrication, delivery, labor, and any required engineering or testing documentation. That's the number we're going to focus on here.

These ranges reflect Florida market pricing as of 2026 for competitively bid commercial projects. They are not list prices or catalog figures - they're what you should expect to see in a real subcontractor proposal.

Glass Type Pricing: Cost Per Square Foot

Glass type is only one component of your total installed cost - the framing system usually costs more than the glass itself - but understanding glass pricing helps you make informed decisions when value-engineering a spec.

Note that these are glass-only figures. The framing system - storefront, curtainwall, or window wall - adds substantially to the installed cost. More on that below.

Storefront vs. Curtainwall: Installed System Cost

The single biggest cost driver in a commercial glazing scope is system selection. Storefront, curtainwall, and window wall are not interchangeable - they perform differently, install differently, and cost very differently.

Commercial Storefront: Installed

Storefront is a stick-built aluminum framing system designed for low-rise applications - typically single-story or limited-height, non-structural facades. It's the most common glazing system on retail, medical office, restaurant, and single-story commercial projects.

The low end of that range applies to straightforward, single-story scopes with standard aluminum and clear glass. The high end applies to larger projects with architectural finishes, custom colors, monumental heights, or impact-rated glass requirements.

Curtainwall: Installed

Curtainwall is a non-load-bearing exterior wall system that spans floor-to-floor and is engineered to handle wind loads, thermal movement, and water infiltration at high-rise scale. It's substantially more expensive than storefront for good reason - the engineering, testing, shop drawing complexity, and installation labor are all significantly greater.

A standard 2" x 4" stick-built curtainwall on a mid-rise office building sits near the lower end of that range. A custom unitized system on a trophy project with specialty glass, deep shadow boxes, and complex geometry sits at the high end - or above it.

See our services page for a full breakdown of the curtainwall systems ACG installs.

Window Wall: Installed

Window wall sits between storefront and curtainwall in both cost and performance. It's a system where the glazing assembly spans floor-to-floor but sits in front of the concrete slab rather than bypassing it. It's common in mid-rise multifamily construction - and it's increasingly popular because it performs well at a price point that makes sense for residential-over-podium projects.

The HVHZ Impact Premium: What It Costs to Build in Miami-Dade and Broward

Florida's High Velocity Hurricane Zone (HVHZ) - covering Miami-Dade and Broward counties - requires glazing products to pass the most rigorous wind and impact testing in the country. Every glazing product installed in the HVHZ must carry a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance (NOA) documenting that it passed large-missile impact testing and cyclic pressure testing per the applicable test protocols.

What does that mean for cost? The premium comes from several sources:

Product selection narrows significantly. Not every manufacturer's product line has HVHZ NOAs. You're choosing from a smaller menu of tested, approved products - and those products command a market premium.

Documentation and submittal requirements are more extensive. An HVHZ shop drawing submittal is larger, more detailed, and requires more engineering review than a standard Florida submittal. That adds to subcontractor overhead.

Inspection requirements are stricter. Miami-Dade inspectors are looking specifically for correct NOA products, correct anchor patterns, and correct installation sequences. Failed inspections are expensive. A subcontractor with HVHZ experience prices in the cost of getting it right the first time.

Factors That Move Your Number Up or Down

Within any given system type, a dozen variables affect where your actual number lands in the range. Here are the most significant ones:

Glass Specification

The difference between clear tempered and a high-performance triple-silver Low-E IGU in an impact-rated laminate package is substantial. Energy code compliance, tenant comfort, and architectural intent all drive glass selection - and glass selection directly drives cost. Specifying more performance than your project needs is an easy place to spend unnecessary budget.

Frame Finish and Color

Standard clear anodize or bronze anodize aluminum finishes are priced into every standard storefront and curtainwall product line. Custom colors - especially fluoropolymer (Kynar) painted finishes in non-standard colors - add cost.

Project Scale

Larger projects benefit from economies of scale. A 500 SF storefront scope on a single building is more expensive per square foot than a 5,000 SF scope on the same building type. Mobilization, setup, and shop drawing costs are relatively fixed - they don't scale linearly with square footage.

Access and Logistics

Second-floor and above glazing, swing-stage or man-lift work, tight urban sites, and phased-occupancy projects all add cost. If your project has unusual access requirements, your glazing sub needs to know about them at bid time - not as a change order discussion later.

Lead Times and Schedule Pressure

Commercial glazing lead times from major manufacturers in 2026 run 10-20 weeks for standard products and longer for custom specifications. Projects with aggressive schedules may require premium sourcing, expedited fabrication, or split-shipment logistics - all of which add cost. Plan your glazing procurement timeline early. How to Get an Accurate Scope for Your Project

The ranges in this guide will get you to a realistic ballpark for budgeting - but they will not give you a precise number for a specific project. The only way to get that number is to put your drawings in front of a qualified glazing subcontractor.

What you should provide: architectural floor plans, elevations, and glazing schedules. What you should expect in return: a line-item scope breakdown by system type, a square footage takeoff, system recommendations, and a base bid with clear alternates. A qualified glazing sub should be able to turn that around within 48 hours of receiving complete drawings.
