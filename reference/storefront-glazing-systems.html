What is a storefront glazing system?

A plain explanation of the assembly, how it differs from a window and from curtainwall, and what Florida code requires before it can be installed.

1.01The assembly

Walk into a bank, a coffee shop, or an office lobby. The entire front wall is glass, there is a glass door in it, and the panels run from the floor to the ceiling. That assembly has a name: a storefront glazing system.

Three components make it up:

  • Aluminum framing. The extruded grid that carries the glass and anchors to the structure.
  • Glass infill. Fixed lites filling each opening in the grid.
  • Entrances. Swing or sliding door leaves in matching aluminum.

Together they form a wall made mostly of glass: it admits light, resists weather, and carries no load other than its own weight and the wind.

1.02Where it is used

Storefront is the most common glazed assembly in commercial construction. It appears on banks and credit unions, restaurants and coffee shops, office lobbies, retail in strip centers, gyms and fitness studios, medical and urgent-care offices, and dealership showrooms.

If a building has a glass front wall with a glass door in it, it is almost certainly a storefront system.

1.03How it differs from windows

A window is an individual unit set into its own opening. The wall around it is still solid, and the wall is what carries the load.

A storefront system replaces the wall. The aluminum framing is the structure of that plane and the glass fills it. There is nothing behind the glass. Frame and glass run floor to ceiling.

That distinction is what raises the stakes. The framing has to anchor to building structure, and the assembly has to meet the wind and impact requirements for its specific location, elevation, and jurisdiction.

1.04How it differs from curtainwall

Curtainwall does the same basic job at a larger scale. The difference is how it meets the building.

AssemblyRelationship to structurePractical distinction
Storefront Typically infills a prepared wall opening and carries glass, framing, and wind loads through perimeter anchors into the adjacent structure. Non-load-bearing, and it usually stops at a head, sill, or intermediate slab. Common on punched openings and one-story bays. Standard framing often runs about 4-1/2 to 6 inches deep, but available depths, reinforcements, spans, and pressures vary by manufacturer and by approval.
Curtainwall A continuous non-load-bearing exterior wall supported at the building structure, commonly at the floor slabs, with anchors and joints arranged to carry dead load and take up building movement. Commonly spans floor to floor and can run across many stories. Stick-built or unitized. Selected when continuity, span, movement, drainage, or loading exceed what a storefront system is documented to do.

What actually decides the classification is the load path, the support and anchorage, the span, the drainage concept, and the system's tested or engineered limits. It is not a height rule. A tall single-story storefront bay can be within an approved system, and a low building can still call for curtainwall if the load path runs through the slab edge.

Note

MasterFormat commonly places entrances and storefronts in 08 41 00, and curtain wall and glazed assemblies in 08 44 00. The written specification, not the section number, is what assigns delegated design, calculations, submittals, and testing.

1.05Performance criteria

Storefront systems are not interchangeable. Storefront is selected as a complete, compatible system for a specific project, and the basis of design has to name the numbers it has to hit:

  • Design pressures. Positive and negative, from the project's wind loads, exposure, risk category, and opening elevation.
  • Air and water performance. Air infiltration and water penetration resistance, plus the structural capacity and deflection criteria the framing has to hold.
  • Glass makeup and compatible framing. Glass, framing, entrances, and anchor conditions have to be a documented set, not parts assembled from different systems.
  • Impact protection where it applies. In a wind-borne debris region, use impact-resistant glazing or an approved protective system tested and approved for the required elevation, design pressure, configuration, and jurisdiction.

Thermal breaks, insulating glass, and Low-E coatings get selected where they are needed to meet the applicable energy code path and the project's performance requirements. They are not universal storefront requirements, and a spec that treats them as automatic is a spec that has not been priced.

Code reference

Large-missile impact and cyclic pressure testing under ASTM E1886 and E1996 drives the impact rating. The Florida Building Code requires impact-resistant glazing or approved protection in wind-borne debris regions, with the large-missile requirement generally applying to glazed openings within 30 feet of grade. Miami-Dade and Broward are the High-Velocity Hurricane Zone: select a current Florida Product Approval that is approved for HVHZ use, a Miami-Dade Notice of Acceptance, or another approval the authority having jurisdiction accepts. Verify the approval's code edition, impact classification, design-pressure limits, glass, anchors, and installation details against the actual project.

1.06Permitting

Assume it is permitted work and confirm the requirement with the authority having jurisdiction before anything is ordered. Who files the permit is set by the project and the jurisdiction, not by a rule of thumb. On GC-led commercial work the applicant may be the general contractor, the owner, or an authorized contractor.

For the selected exterior system, submit the current approval or other documentation the jurisdiction accepts, the manufacturer's installation instructions, the project design pressures, and sealed engineering where it is required. Install only within the approval's stated limits and the permitted documents.

Approval drawings identify the configurations and installation limitations that were actually evaluated for that system. Compare the selected glass, framing, anchors, fasteners, substrate, and design pressures against the approval and the permitted shop drawings. Anything outside those limits needs project-specific engineering. That comparison is the whole value of a permitted storefront, and it is where most field problems start.

1.07Who installs it

A qualified commercial glazing contractor with documented experience in the specified system and the specific jurisdiction. Before award, confirm the required licensing, insurance, manufacturer authorization, permit responsibility, and who is coordinating inspections. A contractor who cannot answer those five things on a phone call has not done the work before.
